\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}

Autonomous quadcopters are increasingly deployed in applications that require reliable operation
under varying and uncertain conditions, such as inspection, delivery, or search and rescue.
A fundamental challenge in this context is that real-world flight is subject to disturbances
and parametric variations that are difficult to model precisely: wind gusts, uneven payloads,
sensor noise, or mechanical wear can significantly degrade the performance of a controller
designed under nominal assumptions.

Classical control approaches, such as proportional-integral-derivative (PID) cascades, are
widely used in practice due to their simplicity and robustness. However, their performance
degrades when the system deviates significantly from the conditions under which they were
tuned, and re-tuning for each new scenario is time-consuming and requires expert knowledge.
Learning-based approaches offer a more flexible alternative, as they can in principle adapt
to complex and unknown dynamics from data. Yet standard training procedures produce a fixed
controller that is no more adaptable than its classical counterpart once deployed: it cannot
efficiently incorporate the few observations available at runtime to adjust its behavior.

Meta-learning addresses this limitation by shifting the training objective from learning a
single high-performing policy to learning a policy initialization that can be rapidly
fine-tuned to a new task from a small number of gradient steps~\cite{finn2017maml}.
Applied to quadcopter control, this paradigm promises controllers that maintain good
performance not only in nominal conditions but also after fast in-flight adaptation to
previously unseen disturbances.

A second fundamental challenge, largely orthogonal to the controller design itself, is the
\emph{sim-to-real gap}: controllers trained in simulation rarely transfer directly to hardware
because the simulated dynamics do not exactly match those of the physical system.
Closing this gap requires a simulation environment that is faithful to the real platform,
which in turn requires careful modeling of the onboard control architecture.

\subsection{Prior Work}

This project builds directly on the work of C. Goffin~\cite{goffin2026}, who investigated the
application of Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) to neural network quadcopter controllers
in the preceding semester. Goffin formulated the control problem as the regulation of a
quadcopter to the origin in the presence of wind disturbances modeled as additive Gaussian
noise on the translational dynamics. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) policy was trained by differentiating a
cumulative quadratic cost through an unrolled simulation horizon, with the full control input
$(F, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z)$ as the network output. Both a linearized and a nonlinear
dynamics model were considered, and MAML was shown to outperform joint training when the
test disturbance distribution differs from the training one. All experiments were conducted
in simulation using a custom Python dynamics model; no real hardware was involved.

\subsection{Contributions}

The present work extends Goffin's framework in two complementary directions: making the
simulation faithful to a specific real platform, and deploying the resulting controllers
on actual hardware. The contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Simulation environment selection and validation.}
    The \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} library~\cite{panerati2021} is adopted as the
    simulation backend and its physics modes are compared against Goffin's dynamics model
    in open loop to verify consistency.

    \item \textbf{Crazyflie firmware integration.}
    The default PID controller provided by \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} does not match
    the cascade PID architecture embedded in the Crazyflie firmware. The official
    Crazyflie C firmware is ported to Python and integrated into the simulator, enabling
    closed-loop simulations that closely replicate the real drone's control pipeline.

    \item \textbf{Sim-to-real validation.}
    The fidelity of the simulation is evaluated experimentally by commanding the real
    Crazyflie to follow a circular trajectory and comparing the resulting flight with the
    simulated equivalent. Two positioning systems are compared: the optical flow Flowdeck
    and the Lighthouse system, with the latter retained for its superior accuracy.

    \item \textbf{Hybrid NN-PID architecture and training.}
    Because the Crazyflie firmware does not expose direct motor RPM commands at the
    required bandwidth, the neural network is designed to replace only the outer two levels
    of the cascade PID (position and velocity loops), with its outputs fed into the inner
    attitude and rate loops. This hybrid architecture is trained in simulation and
    evaluated both in simulation and on the real drone.

    \item \textbf{MAML for offset mass compensation.}
    MAML is applied to a disturbance type not considered by Goffin: an offset point mass
    attached to the drone body, which shifts the center of mass and modifies the effective
    inertia tensor. Indeed, the wind disturbance is very difficult to produce and control in
    laboratory, while we can easily stick a mass to the drone. This disturbance is persistent
    and asymmetric, making fast adaptation particularly valuable. The meta-trained controller
    is evaluated in simulation and on the real platform.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Quadcopter Dynamics}\label{sec:dynamics}

\subsubsection*{Reference frames}

Two right-handed reference frames are used throughout the report and are illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:quadcopter}. The inertial (world) frame $\mathcal{W} = (x_I, y_I, z_I)$ is
fixed to the ground, with $z_I$ pointing upwards. The body frame
$\mathcal{B} = (x_B, y_B, z_B)$ is attached to the centre of mass (CoM) of the quadcopter,
with $x_B$ pointing forward, $y_B$ to the left and $z_B$ aligned with the thrust direction.
The orientation of $\mathcal{B}$ with respect to $\mathcal{W}$ is parameterised by the
ZYX Euler angles $\Phi = (\phi, \theta, \psi)^\top$ (roll, pitch, yaw), and the corresponding
rotation matrix that maps body coordinates to world coordinates reads
\begin{equation}
R(\phi,\theta,\psi) =
\begin{bmatrix}
c_\theta c_\psi & s_\phi s_\theta c_\psi - c_\phi s_\psi & c_\phi s_\theta c_\psi + s_\phi s_\psi \\
c_\theta s_\psi & s_\phi s_\theta s_\psi + c_\phi c_\psi & c_\phi s_\theta s_\psi - s_\phi c_\psi \\
-s_\theta      & s_\phi c_\theta                          & c_\phi c_\theta
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:rotation}
\end{equation}
with the shorthand $c_\alpha = \cos\alpha$ and $s_\alpha = \sin\alpha$.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{images/Quadcopter_Picture.png}
    \caption{Quadcopter schematic in the X-configuration adopted in this work. Motors $1$ and
    $3$ rotate counter-clockwise while motors $2$ and $4$ rotate clockwise so that the reaction
    torques cancel in nominal hover. The parameter $l$ denotes the distance between the centre
    of mass and the axis of a motor. Adapted from~\cite[Fig.~2.1]{goffin2026} (originally in
    $+$-configuration) with the motor layout redrawn for the X-configuration used by the
    Crazyflie.}
    \label{fig:quadcopter}
\end{figure}

Compared to the $+$-configuration used by Goffin~\cite{goffin2026}, in which the motors are
aligned with the $x_B$ and $y_B$ axes, the X-configuration places the motors at $45^\circ$
from these axes, which is the standard Bitcraze layout for the Crazyflie. The translational
dynamics are unaffected by this choice, but the expressions of the body torques in terms of
the rotor thrusts differ and are derived below.

\subsubsection*{Translational dynamics}

Let $(x, y, z)^\top$ denote the position of the CoM expressed in $\mathcal{W}$. Each rotor
$i \in \{1,2,3,4\}$ produces a thrust $F_i = k_t \omega_i^2$ along $z_B$, where $\omega_i$ is
its angular speed and $k_t$ is the thrust coefficient. Applying Newton's second law in the
inertial frame yields
\begin{equation}
m \begin{bmatrix} \ddot{x} \\ \ddot{y} \\ \ddot{z} \end{bmatrix}
   = R(\phi,\theta,\psi) \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ F \end{bmatrix}
   - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ m g \end{bmatrix},
\qquad F = \sum_{i=1}^{4} F_i,
\label{eq:translational}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the total mass and $g$ the gravitational acceleration. This expression is
identical to the one in~\cite{goffin2026} and is independent of the motor layout.

\subsubsection*{Rotational dynamics: body rates vs Euler angle derivatives}

A subtle but important point concerns the variable that drives the rotational dynamics.
Goffin~\cite{goffin2026} writes Euler's rigid-body equation directly in terms of the
Euler angle derivatives, i.e.\ $\tau_B = J_B \ddot{\Phi} + \dot{\Phi} \times
(J_B \dot{\Phi})$, and his code propagates the state $(\phi, \dot\phi, \theta, \dot\theta,
\psi, \dot\psi)$ accordingly. This expression is only correct in the small-angle, hover
regime; the exact form involves the body-frame angular velocity $\omega_B = (p, q, r)^\top$,
which is \emph{not} equal to $\dot{\Phi}$ in general. For the sake of rigour and because the
nonlinear simulator used in this work integrates orientation through body rates ($\omega_B$) and
quaternions (see paragraph below and Section~\ref{sec:gym_pybullet}), we briefly re-derive
the rotational equations.

Let $L$ denote the angular momentum of the rigid body about its CoM. The fundamental
theorem of angular momentum reads
\begin{equation}
\tau = \left(\tfrac{d}{dt}\right)_{\!W} L,
\label{eq:fundamental_momentum}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the resultant external torque and $\left(\tfrac{d}{dt}\right)_{W}$ denotes
the time derivative seen by a stationary observer in $\mathcal{W}$. Although $L$ is intrinsic
to the body, its coordinate representation depends on the frame. Expressing it in
$\mathcal{B}$ gives $L_B = J_B \omega_B$ with $J_B = \mathrm{diag}(I_x, I_y, I_z)$ owing to
the symmetry of the quadcopter. Crucially, the inertia tensor expressed in the \emph{world}
frame depends on the current orientation and is therefore time-varying, so one cannot simply
differentiate $J \omega$ in $\mathcal{W}$. The standard remedy is to apply the
transport theorem
\begin{equation}
\left(\tfrac{d}{dt}\right)_{\!W} v = \left(\tfrac{d}{dt}\right)_{\!B} v + \omega \times v,
\end{equation}
valid for any vector $v$, to the angular momentum $L$:
\begin{equation}
\tau_B = \left(\tfrac{d}{dt}\right)_{\!B} L_B + \omega_B \times L_B
       = J_B \dot{\omega}_B + \omega_B \times (J_B \omega_B),
\label{eq:euler_body}
\end{equation}
where the last equality follows from $\left(\tfrac{d}{dt}\right)_{\!B} J_B = 0$. Componentwise,
with $\omega_B = (p, q, r)^\top$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \tau_{Bx} \\ \tau_{By} \\ \tau_{Bz} \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} I_x \dot{p} + (I_z - I_y)\, q r \\
                I_y \dot{q} + (I_x - I_z)\, p r \\
                I_z \dot{r} + (I_y - I_x)\, p q \end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:euler_components}
\end{equation}

The body rates $(p, q, r)$ and the Euler angle derivatives $(\dot\phi, \dot\theta, \dot\psi)$
are related by the well-known kinematic transformation~\cite{goffin2026}
\begin{equation}
\omega_B = W(\phi, \theta) \dot{\Phi}, \qquad
W(\phi, \theta) =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0          & -s_\theta \\
0 & c_\phi     & s_\phi c_\theta \\
0 & -s_\phi    & c_\phi c_\theta
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:kinematic}
\end{equation}
Note that $W(0,0) = I_3$, so that $\omega_B \simeq \dot{\Phi}$ and
$\dot{\omega}_B \simeq \ddot{\Phi}$ near hover. This explains why Goffin's formulation
remains accurate in nominal flight even though it neglects the distinction between body
rates and Euler angle derivatives, and why the resulting \emph{linearised} model coincides
with the one obtained from~\eqref{eq:euler_components}. Outside of the hover regime the
distinction becomes non-negligible; \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones}, used as the simulation
backend in this project, propagates $\omega_B$ directly and integrates the orientation
through a quaternion (see Section~\ref{sec:gym_pybullet}). The empirical agreement between
the two formulations is quantified in the open-loop comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:dyn_comparison}.

\subsubsection*{Motor mixing in X-configuration}

In the X-configuration of Figure~\ref{fig:quadcopter}, the four motors are placed at
$(\pm l/\sqrt{2}, \pm l/\sqrt{2}, 0)$ in $\mathcal{B}$ and each produces a thrust $F_i$
along $z_B$. Computing $r_i \times (F_i \hat z_B)$ for each motor and summing yields
\begin{align}
\tau_{Bx} &= \frac{l}{\sqrt{2}} \left( -F_1 - F_2 + F_3 + F_4 \right), \label{eq:taux_X}\\
\tau_{By} &= \frac{l}{\sqrt{2}} \left( -F_1 + F_2 + F_3 - F_4 \right), \label{eq:tauy_X}\\
\tau_{Bz} &= \frac{k_d}{k_t} \left( F_1 - F_2 + F_3 - F_4 \right), \label{eq:tauz_X}
\end{align}
where $k_d$ is the rotor drag coefficient and the yaw torque~\eqref{eq:tauz_X} accounts
for the alternating direction of rotation of the rotors. The corresponding expressions
in~\cite{goffin2026} for the $+$-configuration involve only two motors per axis
(see equations (2.2)--(2.4) therein); the X-configuration distributes the moment over the
four rotors, which improves actuator authority and is the convention used by the Crazyflie
firmware.

\subsubsection*{Nonlinear continuous-time model}

Combining~\eqref{eq:translational}, \eqref{eq:euler_components}
and~\eqref{eq:taux_X}--\eqref{eq:tauz_X} yields the full nonlinear model
\begin{align}
m \ddot{x} &= F \left( c_\phi s_\theta c_\psi + s_\phi s_\psi \right), \label{eq:nl_x}\\
m \ddot{y} &= F \left( c_\phi s_\theta s_\psi - s_\phi c_\psi \right), \label{eq:nl_y}\\
m \ddot{z} &= F\, c_\phi c_\theta - m g, \label{eq:nl_z}\\
I_x \dot{p} &= \tau_{Bx} - (I_z - I_y)\, q r, \label{eq:nl_p}\\
I_y \dot{q} &= \tau_{By} - (I_x - I_z)\, p r, \label{eq:nl_q}\\
I_z \dot{r} &= \tau_{Bz} - (I_y - I_x)\, p q, \label{eq:nl_r}
\end{align}
NEED TO CHANGE $\tau_{B..}$ because it is used at two places for two different things
completed by an orientation kinematic relation that closes the system: either
$\dot{\Phi} = W(\phi, \theta)^{-1} \omega_B$ from~\eqref{eq:kinematic} when the Euler angles
are kept in the state vector, or the quaternion propagation law
$\dot{q} = \tfrac{1}{2}\, q \otimes \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \omega_B \end{bmatrix}$ when the
orientation is represented by a unit quaternion $q$. The latter choice is the one made by
\texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} in its \texttt{DYN} mode and avoids the singularity of $W$
at $\theta = \pm\pi/2$.

\subsubsection*{Linearisation about hover}

For control design and for comparison with Goffin's model, the system is linearised about
the hovering equilibrium $\phi \simeq \theta \simeq 0$, $\psi = 0$ (without loss of
generality), $\omega_B \simeq 0$ and $F_i = m g / 4$. Under the small-angle approximation
$c_\alpha \simeq 1$, $s_\alpha \simeq \alpha$, the gyroscopic terms in~\eqref{eq:nl_p}--%
\eqref{eq:nl_r} are bilinear in the angular rates and therefore second-order in the
perturbation around $\omega_B = 0$, so they are dropped at first order, and the kinematic
relation~\eqref{eq:kinematic} reduces to $\omega_B \simeq \dot{\Phi}$. The linearised model
then reads
\begin{equation}
\ddot{x} = \frac{F}{m}\, \theta, \quad
\ddot{y} = -\frac{F}{m}\, \phi, \quad
\ddot{z} = \frac{F}{m} - g, \quad
\ddot{\phi} = \frac{\tau_x}{I_x}, \quad
\ddot{\theta} = \frac{\tau_y}{I_y}, \quad
\ddot{\psi} = \frac{\tau_z}{I_z},
\label{eq:linearized}
\end{equation}
which matches~\cite[Eqs.~(2.13)--(2.18)]{goffin2026} up to the sign of the $\ddot y$ equation
that flips because of the left-handed vs.\ right-handed convention on the $y_B$ axis. The
12-dimensional state vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x & \dot{x} & y & \dot{y} & z & \dot{z} &
                              \phi & \dot{\phi} & \theta & \dot{\theta} &
                              \psi & \dot{\psi} \end{bmatrix}^\top
\end{equation}
and the control input $\mathbf{u} = (F, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z)^\top$ are kept as
in~\cite{goffin2026} for consistency.

\subsubsection*{Discretisation and link to the simulator}

For training and simulation the continuous-time dynamics are integrated with a forward
Euler scheme of step $T_s$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + T_s\, f(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k),
\label{eq:euler_discretization}
\end{equation}
where $f(\cdot)$ collects~\eqref{eq:nl_x}--\eqref{eq:nl_r} together with the kinematic
relation. Two important remarks must be made on the implementation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Goffin's simulator integrates the orientation directly through Euler angles, which
    is equivalent to assuming $\omega_B \equiv \dot{\Phi}$. This shortcut is valid in
    near-hover regimes but introduces an error that grows with the magnitude of the
    Euler angles, and degenerates at $\theta = \pm\pi/2$ (gimbal lock).
    \item \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} (in its \texttt{DYN} mode) propagates the body rates
    $\omega_B$ through~\eqref{eq:euler_components} and integrates the orientation as a unit
    quaternion $q$ to avoid the singularity. The starting point is the standard
    quaternion kinematics
    \begin{equation}
    \dot{q} = \tfrac{1}{2}\, q \otimes \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \omega_B \end{bmatrix},
    \label{eq:quat_kinematics}
    \end{equation}
    where $\otimes$ denotes the Hamilton product. Expanding this product to the right by
    $[0, \omega_B]^\top$ recasts~\eqref{eq:quat_kinematics} as
    $\dot q = \tfrac{1}{2}\, \Omega(\omega_B)\, q$ with the skew-symmetric matrix
    \begin{equation}
    \Omega(\omega_B) = \begin{bmatrix}
    0  &  r & -q &  p \\
    -r &  0 &  p &  q \\
    q  & -p &  0 &  r \\
    -p & -q & -r &  0
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:Omega}
    \end{equation}
    Because $\Omega^2 = -\|\omega_B\|^2 I_4$, the exact solution for $\omega_B$ constant
    over a sampling period $T_s$ is given by the Rodrigues-like closed form
    \begin{equation}
    q_{k+1} = \left( \cos\vartheta \, I_4 + \frac{\sin\vartheta}{\|\omega_B\|}\, \Omega(\omega_B) \right) q_k,
    \qquad \vartheta = \tfrac{T_s}{2}\|\omega_B\|,
    \label{eq:quat_update}
    \end{equation}
    which is precisely the formula implemented in \texttt{\_integrateQ} (a factor $2$
    appears explicitly in the source code to cancel the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ absorbed in the
    local variable \texttt{lambda\_}$\, = \tfrac{1}{2}\Omega(\omega_B)$). The Euler angles
    are then recovered solely for the state output via \texttt{getEulerFromQuaternion}.
    A close inspection of the source code is required to confirm that this is the chosen
    convention, because the variable holding the angular velocity is misleadingly named
    \texttt{rpy\_rates}: despite the name, the gyroscopic coupling
    \texttt{cross(rpy\_rates, J\,@\,rpy\_rates)}, the use
    of~\eqref{eq:quat_update}, and the fact that the variable is rotated by $R$ before
    being passed to PyBullet as a world-frame angular velocity, all unambiguously identify
    it as $\omega_B = (p, q, r)^\top$ and not as $\dot{\Phi}$. The quaternion
    kinematics~\eqref{eq:quat_kinematics} itself is only consistent with body rates,
    since the angular velocity that drives $\dot q$ must be expressed in the body frame,
    not as Euler angle derivatives. Finally, the time integration in
    \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} is not a plain forward Euler scheme but the
    \emph{semi-implicit} (or symplectic) variant: the linear and angular velocities are
    updated first, and the position and quaternion are then integrated using the
    \emph{new} velocities, i.e.\
    \begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{v}_{k+1} = \mathbf{v}_k + T_s\, \mathbf{a}_k, \qquad
    \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + T_s\, \mathbf{v}_{k+1},
    \end{equation*}
    and analogously for $(\omega_B, q)$. This scheme is known to preserve energy on long
    horizons for conservative rigid-body systems much better than the explicit Euler
    discretisation~\eqref{eq:euler_discretization}. This is the physically correct choice
    and is the one used throughout the remainder of this report. TODO WHEN BEGINNING SECTIONS

\end{enumerate}
The quantitative impact of these modelling differences is investigated in the open-loop
benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:dyn_comparison}.

\subsection{Crazyflie Cascade PID Architecture}\label{sec:cascade_pid}

The control stack embedded in the Crazyflie 2.x firmware~\cite{bitcraze_controllers} is a
\emph{cascade of four nested PID loops}, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cascade_pid}. The
cascade structure is the standard solution to the underactuation and time-scale separation of
the quadcopter: the translational dynamics are slow and indirectly actuated through the
attitude of the body, while the rotational dynamics are fast and directly actuated by the
motor thrusts. Each loop is then dedicated to controlling a single physical quantity at the
bandwidth at which it actually evolves, and provides its inner neighbour with a setpoint.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{images/Cascaded_PID.png}
    \caption{Cascade-PID architecture implemented in the Crazyflie firmware. The position
    and velocity loops run at $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$, the attitude and attitude-rate loops at
    $500\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Each PID is fed by the state estimator and produces the setpoint
    for the next inner loop; the rate loop drives the power distribution that converts the
    desired thrust and body torques into per-motor commands. Reproduced
    from~\cite{bitcraze_controllers}.}
    \label{fig:cascade_pid}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{The four loops}

From the outermost to the innermost, the loops carry the following responsibilities.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Position loop} ($100\,\mathrm{Hz}$). Given the desired position
    $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{sp}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and the estimated position
    $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$, an independent PID on each axis computes a desired velocity in the
    world frame
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{sp}} = \mathrm{PID}_{\mathrm{pos}}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{sp}}
    - \hat{\mathbf{p}}\right),
    \end{equation}
    with $\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{sp}}$ clamped to the bounds
    $|v_x^{\mathrm{sp}}|, |v_y^{\mathrm{sp}}|, |v_z^{\mathrm{sp}}| \le 1\,\mathrm{m/s}$
    enforced by the firmware.
    \item \textbf{Velocity loop} ($100\,\mathrm{Hz}$). The estimated velocity
    $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ is first rotated into the body frame, and the velocity error is
    converted by three PIDs into a desired attitude (roll/pitch) and a thrust command:
    \begin{equation}
    (\phi^{\mathrm{sp}}, \theta^{\mathrm{sp}}, T) = \mathrm{PID}_{\mathrm{vel}}\!\left(
    \mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{sp}} - \hat{\mathbf{v}}\right),
    \end{equation}
    with $|\phi^{\mathrm{sp}}|, |\theta^{\mathrm{sp}}| \le 20^\circ$ and $T$ expressed as a
    16-bit PWM-like value centred on a hover baseline. The yaw setpoint $\psi^{\mathrm{sp}}$
    is independent and either commanded directly or integrated from a yaw-rate command. The
    fact that the velocity loop produces an \emph{attitude} setpoint rather than a force is
    a direct consequence of underactuation: lateral motion can only be obtained by tilting
    the thrust vector.
    \item \textbf{Attitude loop} ($500\,\mathrm{Hz}$). Three PIDs on the Euler-angle errors
    produce a desired body-rate vector
    \begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\mathrm{sp}} = \mathrm{PID}_{\mathrm{att}}\!\left(
    \Phi^{\mathrm{sp}} - \hat{\Phi}\right),
    \end{equation}
    where $\Phi^{\mathrm{sp}} = (\phi^{\mathrm{sp}}, \theta^{\mathrm{sp}}, \psi^{\mathrm{sp}})^\top$
    and $\hat{\Phi}$ is the estimated attitude.
    \item \textbf{Attitude-rate loop} ($500\,\mathrm{Hz}$). The innermost PID is fed by the
    raw gyroscope measurements (no estimator in the loop) and produces three signed
    16-bit control signals
    \begin{equation}
    (u_\phi, u_\theta, u_\psi) =
    \mathrm{PID}_{\mathrm{rate}}\!\left(
    \boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\mathrm{sp}} - \boldsymbol{\omega}_B^{\mathrm{meas}}\right),
    \end{equation}
    each saturated to the range $[-2^{15}+1,\, 2^{15}-1]$. These outputs play the role of
    body-frame torque commands -- they are proportional to the desired $\tau_x$, $\tau_y$,
    $\tau_z$ -- but the firmware keeps them in dimensionless integer units that the power
    distribution converts directly into per-motor PWM values; the mapping to physical
    torques in $\mathrm{N\cdot m}$ depends on the motor model and is never made explicit
    onboard. Using the gyroscope rates directly rather than a filtered estimate is critical
    for the bandwidth of this loop, which must reject torque disturbances on a millisecond
    timescale.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Multi-rate scheduling}

Because the four loops are clocked at two different rates, an outer loop refreshes the
setpoint of its inner neighbour less often than that neighbour consumes it. The position
and velocity loops produce a new attitude-and-thrust setpoint once every $10\,\mathrm{ms}$,
whereas the attitude and attitude-rate loops execute every $2\,\mathrm{ms}$. Between two
refreshes the inner cascade simply reuses the most recent outer-loop setpoint unchanged ---
a zero-order hold --- so that each $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$ command is applied for five
consecutive $500\,\mathrm{Hz}$ iterations. The learned controller of
Section~\ref{sec:nn_pid_arch}, which substitutes for the two outer loops, inherits exactly
this scheduling: a single network query is held for five attitude-rate updates.

\subsubsection*{Power distribution}

The thrust $T$ produced by the velocity loop and the rate-loop outputs
$(u_\phi, u_\theta, u_\psi)$ are mapped to per-motor PWM commands $(m_1, m_2, m_3, m_4)$
by the \emph{power distribution} block. With the Crazyflie motor numbering of
Figure~\ref{fig:quadcopter} (M1 front-right, M2 back-right, M3 back-left, M4 front-left,
M1 and M3 clockwise, M2 and M4 counter-clockwise), the firmware uses the linear mixing
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
m_1 &= T - \tfrac{1}{2} u_\phi + \tfrac{1}{2} u_\theta + u_\psi, \\
m_2 &= T - \tfrac{1}{2} u_\phi - \tfrac{1}{2} u_\theta - u_\psi, \\
m_3 &= T + \tfrac{1}{2} u_\phi - \tfrac{1}{2} u_\theta + u_\psi, \\
m_4 &= T + \tfrac{1}{2} u_\phi + \tfrac{1}{2} u_\theta - u_\psi,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:power_distribution}
\end{equation}
followed by a global down-shift if any $m_i$ exceeds the $16$-bit range, so that the
relative attitude commands are preserved at the expense of the absolute thrust level.

\subsubsection*{Time-scale separation and implications for learning}

Running the attitude and attitude-rate loops at $500\,\mathrm{Hz}$ -- five times faster
than the outer loops -- is what allows them to act as a fast disturbance-rejection layer:
when a perturbation such as a wind gust or a sudden mass shift tilts the airframe, the
inner loops can correct the body rates and the attitude on a millisecond timescale, before
the resulting tilt has had time to integrate into a velocity or a position error that the
outer loops would need to correct. The whole architecture is therefore well-conditioned to
reject high-frequency disturbances at the lowest possible level of the cascade.

Two features of this architecture have direct consequences for the design of the
learning-based controller proposed in Section~\ref{sec:nn_pid_arch}. First, the
Crazyflie's radio (CRTP) protocol accepts setpoints at \emph{any} level of the cascade --
position, velocity, attitude, or attitude rate -- but \emph{not} the per-motor PWM values
nor the rate-loop outputs $(u_\phi, u_\theta, u_\psi)$, which remain confined to the
onboard microcontroller. This rules out Goffin's approach of directly emitting
$(F, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z)$ from a neural network: the network has to interface with the
cascade through one of the allowed setpoint types. Second, the time-scale separation
between the outer loops ($100\,\mathrm{Hz}$) and the inner loops ($500\,\mathrm{Hz}$)
makes it natural to replace only the outer two PIDs by a learned controller -- which then
emits velocity or attitude setpoints at $\sim 100\,\mathrm{Hz}$, well within the CRTP
bandwidth -- while keeping the inner attitude and rate loops onboard at
$500\,\mathrm{Hz}$. This architectural choice is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:nn_pid_arch}.

\subsection{Neural Network Control Policy}\label{sec:nn_policy}

Following Goffin~\cite{goffin2026}, the controller synthesised in this project is a
feedforward neural network --- a multilayer perceptron (MLP) --- used as a deterministic
state-feedback policy. Writing $\pi_\theta$ for the network with trainable parameters
$\theta$, the policy maps an observation $\mathbf{o}_k$ of the drone state at discrete
time $k$ to an action $\mathbf{a}_k$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_k = \pi_\theta(\mathbf{o}_k).
\label{eq:policy}
\end{equation}
The network is queried at the $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$ rate of the outer cascade loops
(Section~\ref{sec:cascade_pid}). Its internal architecture --- the number and width of
the hidden layers and the output activations --- is described together with the training
procedure in Section~\ref{sec:nn_pid_arch}. The present section only fixes \emph{what the
network observes} and \emph{what it commands}, as these two choices are where this work
departs most clearly from~\cite{goffin2026}.

\subsubsection*{Observation}

The observation passed to the policy is the $12$-dimensional rigid-body state introduced
in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics},
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{o}_k = \big(\,
\underbrace{x,\;y,\;z}_{\text{position}},\;\;
\underbrace{v_x,\;v_y,\;v_z}_{\text{velocity}},\;\;
\underbrace{\phi,\;\theta,\;\psi}_{\text{attitude}},\;\;
\underbrace{p,\;q,\;r}_{\text{body rates}}
\,\big),
\label{eq:observation}
\end{equation}
augmented with the regulation error $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{sp}} - \hat{\mathbf{p}}$ between
the desired and estimated positions, which is what allows a single trained policy to be
retargeted to an arbitrary setpoint (Section~\ref{sec:nn_pid_arch}).

\begin{rmk}
The angular part of the observation~\eqref{eq:observation} consists of the body rates
$(p, q, r)$, \emph{not} the Euler-angle derivatives $(\dot\phi, \dot\theta, \dot\psi)$.
This is a deliberate and consistent choice across every regime in which the policy is
used. In simulation --- both during training and during closed-loop evaluation --- the
dynamics propagate $(p, q, r)$ as the genuine body-frame angular velocity through the
rotational equations~\eqref{eq:euler_components}; when the policy is run inside
\texttt{gym-pybullet-drones}, the world-frame angular velocity returned by the simulator
is rotated into the body frame by $R^\top$ before being passed to the network. On the
real Crazyflie, the corresponding three entries are filled directly from the raw
gyroscope, which natively measures body rates. The policy is therefore always presented
with the same physical quantity. As shown in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, near hover
$\omega_B \simeq \dot{\Phi}$, so this distinction has no practical effect in the operating
regime of a regulation task; it is nonetheless essential that training and deployment
\emph{agree} on it, since any mismatch here would translate directly into a sim-to-real
discrepancy. The Euler angles $(\phi, \theta, \psi)$ themselves enter the observation
unchanged; the only convention adjustment concerns the pitch axis, as the Crazyflie
firmware reports a positive pitch for a nose-up attitude whereas the simulator adopts the
opposite sign --- the pitch angle $\theta$ and pitch rate $q$ are therefore negated when
the observation is assembled from real telemetry.
\end{rmk}

\subsubsection*{Action}

The most significant departure from~\cite{goffin2026} lies in the action. Goffin's network
emitted the full control wrench $(F, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z)$ directly, which is the
natural input of an idealised dynamics model. As established in
Section~\ref{sec:cascade_pid}, this is not feasible on the Crazyflie: the CRTP radio
protocol exposes no per-motor or rate-loop command, and the attitude-rate loop must in any
case run at a bandwidth far higher than a network can be queried over radio. The policy
therefore outputs a \emph{setpoint for the inner cascade} rather than a force. Concretely,
the action is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_k = \big(\, T,\;\; \phi^{\mathrm{sp}},\;\; \theta^{\mathrm{sp}},\;\;
r^{\mathrm{sp}} \,\big),
\label{eq:action}
\end{equation}
that is, precisely the quantities produced by the velocity loop of the firmware cascade
(Section~\ref{sec:cascade_pid}): a thrust command $T$ as a $16$-bit PWM-like value, a
desired roll angle $\phi^{\mathrm{sp}}$, a desired pitch angle $\theta^{\mathrm{sp}}$, and
a desired yaw rate $r^{\mathrm{sp}}$. The thrust is forwarded to the power distribution,
the roll and pitch setpoints feed the attitude loop, and the yaw rate is integrated into a
yaw-angle setpoint that also feeds the attitude loop. In effect, the network replaces the
position and velocity loops of the cascade, while the attitude and attitude-rate loops are
left untouched and continue to run onboard at $500\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Each component of the
action is bounded by construction through the output activations --- a sigmoid for the
thrust and hyperbolic tangents for the three angular setpoints --- so that the commands
always lie within the firmware-enforced saturation limits stated in
Section~\ref{sec:cascade_pid} (in particular $|\phi^{\mathrm{sp}}|,
|\theta^{\mathrm{sp}}| \le 20^\circ$).

This hybrid neural-network/PID interface is shared by both controllers studied in this
report --- the classic regulation policy of Section~\ref{sec:nn_pid_arch} and the
meta-learned policy of Section~\ref{sec:maml_bg} --- and is identical in simulation and on
hardware. The detailed network architecture realising the
mapping~\eqref{eq:policy}--\eqref{eq:action} is presented in
Section~\ref{sec:nn_pid_arch}.

\subsection{Back-Propagation Through Time and Curriculum Reset}\label{sec:bptt}

The neural network controller of Section~\ref{sec:nn_policy} is trained by directly
minimising a cost accumulated along a simulated flight. Every component of the closed loop
--- the policy, the inner attitude and attitude-rate PID loops of
Section~\ref{sec:cascade_pid}, and the rigid-body dynamics --- is implemented as a
differentiable operation, so the entire roll-out forms a single differentiable
computational graph and the gradient of the cost with respect to the network parameters
can be obtained exactly by automatic differentiation. This section introduces the two
ingredients of the training procedure that are independent of the particular cost function
used: the back-propagation-through-time mechanism and the curriculum reset. The stage cost
$\ell(\cdot)$ is kept abstract here; its concrete form is specified later, together with
the rest of the training set-up, in Section~\ref{sec:nn_training}.

\subsubsection*{Back-propagation through time}

Consider a roll-out of horizon $N$. Starting from an initial state $\mathbf{x}_0$, at each
step $k$ the policy produces an action $\mathbf{a}_k = \pi_\theta(\mathbf{o}_k)$, and the
inner PID loops together with the dynamics map $(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{a}_k)$ to the next
state $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$. A cumulative cost
\begin{equation}
J(\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \ell(\mathbf{x}_k)
\label{eq:cumulative_cost}
\end{equation}
is accumulated over the trajectory, where $\ell(\cdot)$ is a per-step stage cost. Since
$\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ depends on $\mathbf{x}_k$, which itself depends on the earlier action
$\mathbf{a}_{k-1} = \pi_\theta(\mathbf{o}_{k-1})$, the parameters $\theta$ influence the
cost through \emph{every} step of the roll-out. Unrolling this recurrence and applying the
chain rule backwards from $J$ to $\theta$ is exactly \emph{back-propagation through time}
(BPTT): the same algorithm used to train recurrent neural networks, applied here to the
unrolled simulator rather than to a recurrent cell. Goffin~\cite{goffin2026} already trains
his controller in this way, differentiating the cumulative cost through the unrolled
horizon, but does not name the mechanism; it is made explicit here.
Because the dynamics model is itself differentiable, the resulting gradient is an
\emph{analytic policy gradient}~\cite{wiedemann2023apg}: in contrast with the
score-function estimators used in model-free reinforcement learning, it is exact (up to
floating-point precision) and has low variance, which is what makes training from a small
number of trajectories practical. The unrolled training loop used in this work is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:bptt}.

    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/BPTT.png}
    \caption{Unrolled training loop and back-propagation through time. At each step the
    neural network maps the current state and the relative position target
    $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{sp}} - \hat{\mathbf{p}}$ to a desired attitude and thrust; the

    cumulative loss, and the gradient is back-propagated through the entire unrolled chain
    --- network, PID loops and dynamics --- to update the network weights. Only the network

    \label{fig:bptt}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{Concurrent versus autoregressive prediction}

The analytic-policy-gradient framework of Wiedemann et al.~\cite{wiedemann2023apg},
followed here, additionally proposes a \emph{concurrent} prediction scheme in which the
network is queried once at the start of a chunk of $T$ steps and outputs the whole sequence
of $T$ future actions at once, the chunk then being executed open-loop before the next
query. The two extremes are $T = 1$, where the policy is re-evaluated on fresh state
feedback at every step --- the classical \emph{autoregressive} scheme --- and large $T$,
which amortises the query cost but sacrifices within-chunk feedback. Both settings were
implemented and compared in this work. The best regulation performance was consistently
obtained with $T = 1$, presumably because position regulation on a light and fast
quadcopter benefits more from tight state feedback than from open-loop look-ahead. The
concurrent variant was therefore abandoned, and all controllers reported in this work use
the autoregressive setting.

\subsubsection*{Curriculum reset}

Unrolling a long horizon with a poorly trained policy is problematic: early in training the
controller lets the drone diverge, the state magnitudes grow, and the gradient
back-propagated through the divergent trajectory explodes or becomes numerically invalid.
The \emph{curriculum reset}, adopted from~\cite{wiedemann2023apg}, addresses this. During
the roll-out, after every step, any trajectory whose position error exceeds a threshold
$\tau_{\mathrm{div}}$ is reset: its state, and the internal state of the inner PID loops,
are set back to the regulation target and --- crucially --- \emph{detached} from the
computational graph, so that no gradient flows through the divergence. The detachment
serves two purposes. First, it truncates BPTT for the trajectories that have left the
useful region, keeping the gradient finite, while the well-behaved trajectories remain
fully differentiable. Second, and more subtly, it removes a perverse incentive: were the
reset part of the differentiable graph, the gradient would expose it as a shortcut to a low
cost, since diverging fast enough triggers a reset that snaps the drone straight onto the
regulation target. The policy would then be rewarded for diverging on purpose rather than
for regulating; detaching the reset is what prevents this.

The threshold itself follows a curriculum: it increases linearly with the training epoch
$e$ from a tight initial value $\tau_{\mathrm{start}}$ to a loose final value
$\tau_{\mathrm{end}}$,
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{div}}(e) = \tau_{\mathrm{start}}
+ \left(\tau_{\mathrm{end}} - \tau_{\mathrm{start}}\right) \frac{e}{E - 1},
\qquad e \in \{0, \dots, E-1\},
\label{eq:tau_curriculum}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the total number of epochs. Early in training the policy is poor, so a tight threshold resets diverging
drones quickly and confines the roll-out to a neighbourhood of the target where the
half-trained policy can still produce a meaningful gradient. As the policy improves, the
threshold is relaxed, progressively exposing the controller to longer excursions and to the
recovery-from-far regime. The scheme thus realises an easy-to-hard curriculum; it is
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:curric_reset}.

    \caption{Curriculum reset, reproduced from~\cite{wiedemann2023apg}. In the original

\subsection{Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML)}\label{sec:maml_bg}

Meta-learning, often referred to as \emph{learning to learn}, aims at training
models that can rapidly adapt to new tasks from only a handful of observations,
by exploiting the structure shared across a family of related tasks. Several
methodological families have been proposed in the literature: \emph{model-based}
methods rely on recurrent or memory-augmented architectures, \emph{metric-based}
methods learn an embedding space in which new samples can be efficiently
compared to previously seen ones, and \emph{optimisation-based} methods learn an
initialisation tuned for fast gradient-based adaptation. The present work
adopts an optimisation-based method, namely Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning
(MAML)~\cite{finn2017maml}; this mirrors the choice made by
Goffin~\cite{goffin2026} in the preceding semester, both for direct
comparability and because MAML is particularly well suited to closed-loop
control: the rapidly-adaptable initialisation it produces can be fine-tuned
online from a few real-world trajectories after deployment, without requiring
any change to the controller architecture.

\subsubsection*{Meta-training}

MAML assumes a distribution of tasks $p(\mathcal{T})$, where each task
$\mathcal{T}_i$ is equipped with its own loss function
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}$ -- the cumulative trajectory
cost~\eqref{eq:cumulative_cost} in this work. The goal is to learn a parameter
initialisation $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ from which a small number of gradient
steps on any task drawn from $p(\mathcal{T})$ yields an adapted parameter set
that performs well on that task. To this end, $m$ tasks
$\{\mathcal{T}_i\}_{i=1}^{m}$ are sampled from $p(\mathcal{T})$, and
meta-training proceeds as an alternation of two nested loops, illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:maml_schematic} and summarised in Algorithm~\ref{alg:maml}.

In the \emph{inner loop}, each task $\mathcal{T}_i$ is presented with a small
``support'' batch of $K_{\mathrm{inner}}$ samples, on which one (or a few)
gradient steps of the task loss produce task-adapted parameters
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_i' \;=\; \boldsymbol{\theta}
- \alpha \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}\!\left(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right),
\label{eq:maml_inner}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the inner-loop learning rate and $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$
denotes the controller parameterised by $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. In the
\emph{outer loop}, each adapted controller is evaluated on a fresh ``query''
batch of $K_{\mathrm{outer}}$ samples drawn from the same task, and the
meta-parameters are updated to minimise the sum of these post-adaptation losses
across all tasks:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta} \;\leftarrow\; \boldsymbol{\theta}
- \beta\, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \sum_{i=1}^{m}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}\!\left(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_i'}\right),
\label{eq:maml_outer}
\end{equation}
with $\beta$ the outer-loop learning rate. The key point
of~\eqref{eq:maml_outer} is that the gradient is taken with respect to the
meta-parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ \emph{before} adaptation, so it
back-propagates through the inner update~\eqref{eq:maml_inner}. In its standard
second-order form this involves the Hessian of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}$
through the chain rule; a first-order approximation that treats the inner
update as identity, originally proposed in~\cite{finn2017maml}, halves the
computational cost and was found to yield indistinguishable results in our
experiments (TO CHECK AT THE END OF THE PROJECT). The meta-loss~\eqref{eq:maml_outer}
explicitly optimises for fast \emph{within-task} adaptation rather than for
average performance across tasks, which is what distinguishes MAML from a
joint-training baseline.

In the canonical formulation of~\cite{finn2017maml} recalled above and
summarised in Algorithm~\ref{alg:maml}, a fresh batch of tasks is drawn from
$p(\mathcal{T})$ at every meta-iteration, and the support and query batches are
re-sampled at each iteration. The concrete sampling protocol adopted in this
work --- a \emph{fixed} task set whose support and query points are drawn once
at start-up and reused at every epoch --- deviates from this canonical
formulation for the sake of reproducibility and of a strictly fair comparison
with the baseline; it is detailed and contrasted with both~\cite{finn2017maml}
and~\cite{goffin2026} in Section~\ref{sec:maml_training}.

\subsubsection*{Few-shot adaptation on a new task}

Once meta-training has converged, the resulting initialisation
$\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is expected to enable rapid adaptation to any new task
$\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{target}} \sim p(\mathcal{T})$. Given $K_{\mathrm{inner}}$
samples drawn from $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{target}}$, the adapted parameters are
obtained by performing exactly the same gradient step as in the inner loop,
possibly repeated a few times:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{final}} \;=\; \boldsymbol{\theta}
- \alpha \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{target}}}\!\left(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right),
\label{eq:maml_adapt}
\end{equation}
and the controller $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{final}}}$ is then deployed.
Adaptation thus uses the very same machinery as training, with no retraining
from scratch and no modification of the network architecture; the cost of
deploying on a new task is reduced to a few backward passes through the
unrolled rollout.

\subsubsection*{Joint-training baseline}

The natural baseline against which the meta-learned controller will be compared
in Section~\ref{sec:maml_results} is a controller trained jointly on all $m$
tasks, i.e.\ by minimising the average loss
$\sum_{i=1}^{m} \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$ with
standard gradient descent until convergence. This baseline has no notion of
fast adaptation: it learns one set of parameters that is a compromise across
the entire training distribution. To make the comparison fair, the same
few-shot adaptation~\eqref{eq:maml_adapt} is applied to the jointly-trained
controller at test time (and, in this work, also during training to monitor a
target-task generalisation metric in a symmetric fashion -- see
Section~\ref{sec:maml_training}). Any performance gap between the two then
reflects the specific contribution of the meta-optimisation: whether the
meta-trained initialisation truly enables faster gradient-based adaptation than
an initialisation obtained from averaging across tasks.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{MAML meta-training and few-shot adaptation (canonical
formulation~\cite{finn2017maml}). The fixed-task-set variant used in this work
is described in Section~\ref{sec:maml_training}.}
\label{alg:maml}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Task distribution $p(\mathcal{T})$; inner LR $\alpha$; outer LR $\beta$;
         support/query sizes $K_{\mathrm{inner}}, K_{\mathrm{outer}}$;
         meta-batch size $m$.
\State Initialise meta-parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ randomly.
\While{not converged}
    \State Sample a batch of $m$ tasks $\{\mathcal{T}_i\}_{i=1}^{m} \sim p(\mathcal{T})$.
    \For{$i = 1$ to $m$}
        \State Draw $K_{\mathrm{inner}}$ support samples from $\mathcal{T}_i$.
        \State $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i' \gets \boldsymbol{\theta}
               - \alpha\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,
               \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$
               \Comment{inner update~\eqref{eq:maml_inner}, on the support batch}
        \State Draw $K_{\mathrm{outer}}$ query samples from $\mathcal{T}_i$.
        \State Evaluate $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}\!\left(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_i'}\right)$
               on the query batch.
    \EndFor
    \State $\boldsymbol{\theta} \gets \boldsymbol{\theta}
           - \beta\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}
           \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}\!\left(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_i'}\right)$
           \Comment{meta-update~\eqref{eq:maml_outer}}
\EndWhile
\Statex
\State \textbf{Few-shot adaptation on an unseen $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{target}} \sim p(\mathcal{T})$:}
\State Draw $K_{\mathrm{inner}}$ samples from $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{target}}$.
\State $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{final}} \gets \boldsymbol{\theta}
       - \alpha\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,
       \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{target}}}(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$
       \Comment{can be iterated for $n_{\mathrm{adapt}} > 1$ steps}
\State \Return $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{final}}$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Simulation Environment}

A faithful simulation environment is the prerequisite for both training the
controllers of Sections~\ref{sec:nn_pid_arch} and~\ref{sec:maml_training} and for
closing the sim-to-real gap discussed in Section~\ref{sec:simreal}. This chapter
introduces the simulation backend adopted in this work
(Section~\ref{sec:gym_pybullet}), quantifies its agreement with Goffin's dynamics
model in open loop (Section~\ref{sec:dyn_comparison}), and describes how the
Crazyflie firmware was ported into it to obtain a realistic closed-loop pipeline
(Section~\ref{sec:firmware_integration}).

\subsection{gym-pybullet-drones}\label{sec:gym_pybullet}

The simulation backend used throughout this project is
\texttt{gym-pybullet-drones}~\cite{panerati2021}, an open-source quadcopter
simulator built on the Bullet physics engine through its Python binding
PyBullet~\cite{coumans2021pybullet} and exposing the standard Gymnasium
environment interface. The library ships with a validated model of the
Bitcraze Crazyflie 2.x --- the platform targeted in this project --- and was
developed precisely for learning-based multi-agent quadcopter control, which
makes it a natural fit for the present work.

\subsubsection*{Choice of simulator}

The main alternative considered was Webots~\cite{michel2004webots}, a mature
general-purpose robotics simulator that also provides a Crazyflie model. Webots
was discarded in favour of \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} for three reasons that
are decisive given the training method of Section~\ref{sec:bptt}. First, the
training procedure relies on \emph{back-propagation through time} across an
unrolled rollout, which requires the entire closed loop --- policy, inner PID
loops and dynamics --- to be expressed as differentiable operations in the same
automatic-differentiation framework (PyTorch) as the network. What is needed is
therefore a simulator whose dynamics are transparent enough to be
\emph{re-implemented} as differentiable PyTorch tensor operations; the explicit
\texttt{DYN} dynamics of \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} (below) are exactly that,
and the controllers are in fact trained on such a PyTorch port. Webots, by
contrast, couples the controller to an opaque C/C++ physics core through an
external supervisor interface that cannot be differentiated through. Second,
\texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} is
lightweight and scriptable: thousands of short rollouts can be run headlessly
per training epoch from a single Python process, without the overhead of a
full graphical robotics environment. Third, it directly exposes an
\emph{explicit} dynamics mode (described below) whose equations coincide with
the rigid-body model derived in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} and used by
Goffin~\cite{goffin2026}, which both guarantees comparability with the prior
work and gives full control over every term of the model.

\subsubsection*{Physics modes}

\texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} decouples two frequencies: the physics rate at which
the simulation is advanced (default $240\,\mathrm{Hz}$) and the control rate at
which a new control action is applied, the former being an integer multiple of
the latter so that each action is held for a fixed number of physics sub-steps.
The way the physics step is computed is selected through a physics-mode flag, of
which the library offers two families:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{PYB} --- the per-rotor thrusts and the reaction torques are
    applied as external wrenches to the rigid body and the state is advanced by
    the full Bullet constraint solver. Variants
    \texttt{PYB\_GND}, \texttt{PYB\_DRAG} and \texttt{PYB\_DW} add empirical
    ground-effect, blade-drag and downwash corrections on top of this base
    update.
    \item \texttt{DYN} --- the rigid-body equations of motion are integrated
    explicitly in closed form and the result is written back into PyBullet,
    which is then used merely as a kinematic container and visualiser rather
    than as a physics solver.
\end{itemize}
A third mode, \texttt{DYN\_OFFSET}, is a contribution of this project: it
extends \texttt{DYN} with an attached offset point mass --- a shifted centre of
mass and a Steiner-corrected inertia tensor --- and is the mode used for the
MAML experiments of Section~\ref{sec:maml_offset}; it is described in detail in
Section~\ref{sec:maml_task}.

\subsubsection*{The DYN mode}

The explicit \texttt{DYN} mode is central to this project. It is the mode used for
the high-fidelity, closed-loop \emph{sim-to-real validation} of
Section~\ref{sec:simreal} --- in closed loop it is nearly indistinguishable from
\texttt{PYB} --- and it implements exactly the nonlinear model of
Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, which makes it the natural reference for the dynamics
comparison of Section~\ref{sec:dyn_comparison}. The controllers themselves are not
trained inside \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} but on a PyTorch re-implementation of
these dynamics, in the simpler analytic form of Goffin's model (the two forms are
shown to agree in Section~\ref{sec:dyn_comparison}). We describe the \texttt{DYN}
update here, as it underlies both uses. Given the four rotor speeds, the
per-rotor thrusts $F_i = k_t\omega_i^2$ and drag torques $k_d\omega_i^2$ are
combined into the body force and the body torques through the
X-configuration motor mixing of Equations~\eqref{eq:taux_X}--\eqref{eq:tauz_X},
the gyroscopic coupling $\omega_B \times (J_B\omega_B)$ of
Equation~\eqref{eq:euler_components} is subtracted, and the linear and angular
accelerations are obtained from Newton's and Euler's equations. The state is then
advanced with the \emph{semi-implicit} (symplectic) Euler scheme already
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}: the linear and angular velocities are
updated first, after which the position is integrated with the new linear
velocity and the orientation is propagated as a unit quaternion with the new
body rates, using the closed-form update~\eqref{eq:quat_update}. The orientation is represented internally by a quaternion
to avoid the gimbal-lock singularity of the Euler-angle kinematics, and the
Euler angles $(\phi,\theta,\psi)$ are recovered only when the state vector is
assembled for the controller. As established in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, the
angular state propagated by the simulator is the body-rate vector
$\omega_B = (p,q,r)^\top$, not the Euler-angle derivatives; this is the same
quantity observed by the policy~\eqref{eq:observation} and measured by the
gyroscope on the real drone, so the convention is consistent across simulation
and hardware.

\subsection{Dynamics Model Comparison}\label{sec:dyn_comparison}

Before the simulator can be trusted as a training ground, it must be checked
that its dynamics actually reproduce the rigid-body model derived in
Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} and used by Goffin~\cite{goffin2026}. This section
reports an open-loop comparison between \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} and Goffin's
model, in both the nonlinear and the linearized variants, for the
X-configuration of the real Crazyflie and the two physics back-ends
(\texttt{PYB} and \texttt{DYN}).

\subsubsection*{Protocol}

The comparison is run in open loop, so that the only thing being tested is the
dynamics, not a controller. A reference flight is first generated entirely
inside \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones}: its built-in default PID controller flies a
circular trajectory (radius
$R = 0.3\,\mathrm{m}$, period $10\,\mathrm{s}$, hover altitude $1\,\mathrm{m}$) at
a control rate of $48\,\mathrm{Hz}$ over a physics step of $240\,\mathrm{Hz}$.
At every physics sub-step the four motor speeds and the full $12$-dimensional
state are logged, yielding a reference trajectory $\{\mathbf{x}_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\}$
and the corresponding RPM sequence $\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_k\}$. The exact same
RPM sequence is then \emph{replayed} through Goffin's model from the same initial
state, with no feedback, and the two trajectories are compared. Because each
physics mode produces its own reference flight and its own RPM sequence, the
replay is a fair per-mode test of the underlying equations. Two complementary
metrics are used.

\subsubsection*{Open-loop trajectory}

The first metric integrates Goffin's model in free run over the whole
$10\,\mathrm{s}$ circle and overlays the result on the reference
(Figure~\ref{fig:openloop_traj}, X-configuration, \texttt{DYN} mode). Two
features stand out and, at first sight, seem contradictory: the roll and pitch
angles of the nonlinear model stay glued to the reference for the entire flight,
yet its horizontal position tracks well only for about the first fourth of the
circle and then visibly decouples, drifting by more than a metre by
$t = 10\,\mathrm{s}$; the altitude, by contrast, drifts by only about a
centimetre. The linearized model follows the reference for roughly the
first two seconds and then spirals away entirely, as expected from the
small-angle approximation once the integrated error leaves the hover
neighbourhood.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/openloop_cross_dyn_traj.png}
    \caption{Open-loop trajectory comparison for the X-configuration in
    \texttt{DYN} mode. The reference (black) is the closed-loop circular flight
    generated by the DSL cascade PID inside \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones}; the
    nonlinear (blue, dashed) and linearized (red, dotted) Goffin models are
    replayed open-loop from the same initial state with the same logged motor
    commands. Top left: top view in the $x$--$y$ plane; the other panels show
    altitude $z$, roll $\phi$ and pitch $\theta$ versus time. Axis limits track
    the reference, so the linearized model leaves the frame once it diverges.}
    \label{fig:openloop_traj}
\end{figure}

The apparent paradox -- glued attitude but diverging horizontal position -- is a
textbook consequence of open-loop integration rather than a modelling error, and
it follows from the way an attitude error feeds the position. In open loop the
position is obtained by integrating the acceleration \emph{twice}, and near hover
a tilt of the airframe is exactly what creates horizontal acceleration: a pitch
$\theta$ tips the thrust vector forward and gives $\ddot{x} \simeq g\,\theta$,
while a roll gives $\ddot{y} \simeq -g\,\phi$ (the linearised
relations~\eqref{eq:linearized}). A small error $\delta\theta$ on the attitude
therefore acts as a horizontal-acceleration error $g\,\delta\theta$ -- to
\emph{first order} in the attitude error -- which is then integrated twice into
the position.

The attitude error between the two models stays of order $10^{-2}\,\mathrm{rad}$
for the whole flight, a few percent of the plotted range and hence barely visible
on the $\phi$/$\theta$ panels; yet the double time integral turns this almost
invisible error into a horizontal drift of order one metre by
$t = 10\,\mathrm{s}$, concentrated in the second half of the circle simply
because a double integral grows steeply with time. To check that this is the
\emph{entire} story, one can take the attitude error \emph{actually measured
along the run}, convert it into the implied horizontal-acceleration error by
multiplying by $g$, and integrate it twice in time; the result reproduces the
observed position drift to within a few percent ($x$: $0.95\,\mathrm{m}$ from this
double integral vs $0.95\,\mathrm{m}$ observed; $y$: $1.22$ vs
$1.22\,\mathrm{m}$). The horizontal drift is thus, quantitatively, nothing but the
twice-integrated attitude error.

The altitude is spared because the attitude does not enter the vertical
acceleration at first order: $\ddot{z} = (F/m)\cos\phi\cos\theta - g$ depends on
the tilt only through $\cos\phi\cos\theta \simeq 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}(\phi^2+\theta^2)$,
so the same small attitude error perturbs $\ddot{z}$ only at \emph{second} order
in the angles -- a far weaker effect than the first-order horizontal coupling
$g\,\delta\theta$. Its double integral consequently stays at the centimetre level,
about two orders of magnitude below the horizontal drift, as
Figure~\ref{fig:openloop_traj} shows. This is precisely the regime in which an
open-loop trajectory comparison is misleading: it conflates the per-step dynamics
mismatch with its double-integrated accumulation, and the latter -- amplified by
the first-order attitude-to-horizontal-position coupling -- dominates the picture.
The single-step metric introduced next removes this amplification and isolates the
true agreement of the dynamics.

\subsubsection*{Single-step prediction error}

The second metric isolates the dynamics from the accumulation. At every step the
Goffin model is re-initialised to the reference state $\mathbf{x}_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$,
advanced by a \emph{single} step with the logged motor command, and the result is
compared to the next reference state $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{ref}}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_k = f_{\mathrm{Goffin}}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_k^{\mathrm{ref}},
\boldsymbol{\omega}_k\right) - \mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{ref}}.
\label{eq:single_step_err}
\end{equation}
This one-step prediction error is precisely the quantity that matters for the
training procedure of this work: as established in Section~\ref{sec:bptt}, the
best controllers were obtained with an autoregressive (horizon-one) rollout in
which the policy is re-evaluated on fresh state feedback at every step, so the
simulator only ever has to be accurate over a single step. Grouping the twelve
states into position, velocity, attitude and body rates,
Figure~\ref{fig:openloop_singlestep} reports the Euclidean norm of $\mathbf{e}_k$
within each group for the X-configuration in \texttt{DYN} mode, and
Table~\ref{tab:singlestep_rms} collects the root-mean-square error over the whole
trajectory for the three physics back-ends.

\subsubsection*{Interpretation}

The single-step errors decompose cleanly into three contributions, each of which
maps onto a modelling choice discussed in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Position} ($\sim 2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}$ per step,
    identical for both Goffin variants and for both physics modes). The position
    update is purely kinematic, $\mathbf{p}_{k+1} = \mathbf{p}_k +
    \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}$, and the sole difference between the two is which
    velocity is used: \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} integrates with the
    \emph{updated} velocity (semi-implicit Euler) and Goffin with the old one
    (forward Euler). Since the velocity itself agrees to machine precision (next
    item), the single-step position error solely depends on the integration scheme.
    \item \textbf{Velocity.} For the nonlinear model in \texttt{DYN} mode the
    error is at machine precision ($3.8\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm{m/s}$,
    Table~\ref{tab:singlestep_rms}). The translational acceleration
    $\tfrac{F}{m}\,R(\phi,\theta,\psi)\hat{z} - g\hat{z}$ depends on the attitude
    \emph{angles} and the thrust, not on the angular rates -- so the
    body-rate-versus-Euler-rate distinction does not enter this channel at all.
    With the exact (reset) angles and the full thrust projection, which Goffin's
    nonlinear equations~\eqref{eq:nl_x}--\eqref{eq:nl_z} reproduce identically, the
    one-step velocity matches bit for bit. The linearized model instead replaces
    the projection by $g\,\theta$ (resp.\ $-g\,\phi$) and incurs a
    $\sim 6\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ error from the small-angle approximation.
    \item \textbf{Attitude.} The attitude-\emph{angle} error
    ($\sim 5\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}$ per step) is the rotational
    analogue of the position error: exactly as the position update differs only in
    whether the old or the updated \emph{velocity} is integrated, the orientation
    update differs only in whether the old or the updated \emph{body rate} is
    integrated (the updated one in the simulator, the old one in Goffin).
    Whether the orientation is then propagated as Euler angles (Goffin) or as a
    quaternion (the simulator) makes, by itself, no measurable difference.
    \item \textbf{Body rates.} The
    \emph{body-rate} error ($\sim 2\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ per step) is the
    numerical fingerprint of the body-rate-versus-Euler-rate distinction of
    Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}: Goffin advances the angular rate through Euler's
    equation written for the Euler-angle derivatives, whereas the simulator uses
    the body rates $\omega_B$. Rewriting Goffin's rotational equation in the
    body-rate form $J\dot{\omega}_B = \tau_B - \omega_B\times(J\omega_B)$ brings
    this error down to machine precision.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{PYB versus DYN}

Table~\ref{tab:singlestep_rms} also quantifies the effect of the physics
back-end. In \texttt{PYB} mode the nonlinear velocity error rises from machine
precision to $\sim 5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ per step, and adding the
empirical aerodynamic corrections (\texttt{PYB\_GND\_DRAG\_DW}, labelled
\texttt{PYB+aero}) raises it further to $\sim 1.2\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
This is expected: unlike \texttt{DYN}, the general-purpose Bullet constraint
solver does not integrate exactly the same equations, and the aerodynamic models
add genuine physics that Goffin's model omits. The discrepancy nonetheless
stays below the per-step error already incurred by the linearization, so even
\texttt{PYB} stays close to the analytical model. The position, attitude and
body-rate errors are insensitive to the physics mode, as they are governed by the
integration scheme and the angle representation rather than by the force model.
The machine-precision velocity match in \texttt{DYN} mode moreover confirms that
the X-configuration motor mixing of
Equations~\eqref{eq:taux_X}--\eqref{eq:tauz_X} is implemented correctly.

\subsubsection*{Conclusion}

The two models agree to machine precision in the translational channel and to
within a small, bounded and well-understood per-step error in the rotational
channel, the latter being entirely accounted for by the symplectic integration
scheme (attitude angle) and the body-rate versus Euler-rate formulation (angular
rate) discussed in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}. Since training relies on an
autoregressive, single-step rollout (Section~\ref{sec:bptt}), this per-step
agreement is more than sufficient, and on the time scale that actually matters
the two models may be treated as interchangeable.

This justifies the division of labour adopted in the remainder of the report. The
closed-loop \emph{sim-to-real validation} experiments, which must mirror the real
platform as faithfully as possible, are run on the PyBullet model -- in
\texttt{DYN} or \texttt{PYB} mode, the two being nearly indistinguishable in
closed loop, where feedback continually corrects the small open-loop
discrepancies analysed above. \emph{Training}, by contrast, is carried out on a
lightweight PyTorch re-implementation of Goffin's model: back-propagation through
time requires a fully differentiable simulator in the network's own framework,
which the Bullet-based \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} cannot provide, whereas
Goffin's model -- accurate per step and, under closed-loop feedback, accurate
overall -- is both simple to differentiate and faithful enough to train on.

\subsection{Crazyflie Firmware Integration}\label{sec:firmware_integration}

Section~\ref{sec:dyn_comparison} established that the \texttt{DYN} dynamics are
faithful to the real airframe. A closed-loop simulation, however, is only as
realistic as the \emph{controller} that drives those dynamics: if the simulated
drone is flown by a different controller than the physical one, a sim-to-real
comparison can no longer separate a dynamics mismatch from a controller mismatch.
This section explains why the controller shipped with
\texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} is not adequate for this purpose, and how the real
Crazyflie firmware was ported into the simulator to obtain a faithful closed
loop.

\subsubsection*{Why the default controller is not enough}

\texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} ships with a default PID controller, re-tuned at
UTIAS for use inside the simulator. It departs from the on-board
Crazyflie firmware in both structure and gains. Structurally it is a single-rate,
two-stage controller: a position/velocity stage produces a target thrust and
target attitude, and an attitude stage produces a body-torque vector, which a
fixed mixing matrix and an affine PWM-to-RPM fit turn directly into rotor speeds.
It therefore collapses the firmware's four nested loops into two, runs them at a
single rate rather than the firmware's $100/500\,\mathrm{Hz}$ split, and uses
gains (e.g.\ attitude proportional terms of $7\times10^{4}$ in PWM units) that are
chosen to fly well in simulation and bear no relation to the firmware's. Such a
controller is perfectly adequate for generating trajectories --- it is exactly
what was used to drive the open-loop benchmark of
Section~\ref{sec:dyn_comparison} --- but flying the simulated drone with it would
mean the simulated and the real closed loops are governed by different control
laws, which would invalidate any sim-to-real comparison.

\subsubsection*{Porting the firmware}

To remove this discrepancy, the control stack of the official Crazyflie~2.x C
firmware was translated line-by-line into Python. Each routine was kept traceable
to its counterpart in the original firmware source, so that the port can be
audited against it. The translation reproduces:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the firmware's generic PID object, including the details that
    shape the closed-loop response --- derivative taken on the measurement rather
    than the error (to avoid derivative kick), integral clamping, an optional
    two-pole Butterworth low-pass on the derivative term, a feed-forward term, and
    $\pm 180^\circ$ wrapping of the yaw error;
    \item the four cascade stages of Section~\ref{sec:cascade_pid} (position,
    velocity, attitude, attitude-rate), with the exact firmware default gains
    reproduced in Table~\ref{tab:cf_gains};
    \item the firmware's coordinate and bookkeeping conventions: the
    body-yaw-aligned frame in which the position and velocity loops operate, the
    accumulation of the commanded yaw \emph{rate} into a yaw-angle setpoint, the
    integral limits and output saturations of each loop, and the legacy
    quadrotor power distribution with its attitude-priority capping (if a motor
    command exceeds the $16$-bit range, all four are shifted down together so that
    the relative attitude command is preserved at the expense of absolute
    thrust);
    \item the multi-rate scheduling, the position and velocity loops being
    executed once every five attitude/rate updates, exactly as the firmware fires
    its $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$ loops every tenth $1\,\mathrm{kHz}$ tick.
\end{itemize}
The interface also respects the unit and sign conventions of the firmware, which
works in degrees, degrees per second and body-frame gyro rates; in particular the
pitch angle and pitch rate are negated at the boundary, owing to the pitch-sign
difference between PyBullet and the firmware's aerospace convention noted in
Section~\ref{sec:nn_policy}.

\subsubsection*{From motor command to rotor speed}

The real firmware does not output rotor speeds: its power distribution emits four
$16$-bit motor commands, whereas the \texttt{DYN} dynamics are driven by the rotor
speeds $\omega_i$. The bridge therefore reproduces the firmware's actuator path in
two steps. \emph{First}, each $16$-bit command is turned into a per-motor thrust.
The firmware's power distribution defines this command as a \emph{linear} encoding
of the requested per-motor force --- it sets the command to
$\mathrm{force}/F_{\max} \times 65535$, with $F_{\max} \approx 0.12\,\mathrm{N}$
the maximum thrust of one motor --- so the command and the requested thrust are
proportional by construction. \emph{On the real drone}, battery-voltage
compensation (enabled by default) then picks the PWM duty cycle that actually
\emph{delivers} this requested thrust despite the quadratic, battery-dependent
duty-cycle-to-thrust curve of the motor, so that the delivered thrust tracks the
command regardless of the battery's state of charge. \emph{In our simulated
firmware} there is neither a battery nor motor electronics to model, so we keep
only this net command-to-thrust relation and map the command linearly to a
per-motor thrust, $F_i = (\mathrm{command}_i/65535)\,F_{\max}$. \emph{Second}, the
thrust is converted to a rotor speed --- the quantity the
dynamics integrate --- by inverting the rotor thrust model $F_i = k_t\,\omega_i^2$,
i.e.\ $\omega_i = \sqrt{F_i/k_t}$. Finally, the finite spin-up time of the motors
is modelled by a first-order lag on the rotor speed, with a time constant
$\tau \approx 20\,\mathrm{ms}$ --- a value representative of the Crazyflie's small
brushed coreless motors, whose mechanical and electrical response is of the order
of a few tens of milliseconds. With this lag in place, the simulated drone is
actuated essentially as the physical one.

\subsubsection*{Resulting pipeline}

With the ported firmware closing the loop around the \texttt{DYN} dynamics, the
\emph{same} controller now runs in simulation and on the hardware. The sim-to-real
comparison of Section~\ref{sec:simreal} can therefore attribute any residual
discrepancy to the dynamics and the state estimation rather than to the
controller. This pipeline plays two further roles in the rest of the report: its
inner attitude and attitude-rate loops are exactly the ones kept on board when the
neural network replaces the outer position and velocity loops
(Section~\ref{sec:nn_pid_arch}), and the full firmware cascade flies the
takeoff, hover and landing phases that bracket the learned manoeuvre in the
real-world experiments (Section~\ref{sec:nn_results}).

\section{Sim-to-Real Validation}\label{sec:simreal}

Having built a closed-loop simulator that pairs the faithful \texttt{DYN} dynamics
with a Python port of the on-board controller
(Section~\ref{sec:firmware_integration}), we now confront it with the physical
platform. This chapter describes the experimental hardware
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_setup}), compares the two positioning systems that were
available (Section~\ref{sec:flowdeck_lighthouse}), and quantifies the agreement
between simulation and reality on a circular trajectory
(Section~\ref{sec:circle_simreal}).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:exp_setup}

\subsubsection*{Platform}

The hardware is a Bitcraze Crazyflie~2.1+, an open-source nano-quadcopter of about
$29\,\mathrm{g}$ in the X-configuration, driven by four brushed coreless DC motors.
It is described by the same rigid-body model and the same physical quantities --- the
mass $m$, the arm length $l$ and the thrust and drag coefficients $k_t$, $k_m$ --- that
parameterise the dynamics of Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}, and the simulation is configured
with the manufacturer's values for these quantities, so that the simulated and the real
drone share the same nominal dynamics by construction. The drone additionally carries a
positioning deck, mounted on top of or below the frame, of one of the two types compared
in Section~\ref{sec:flowdeck_lighthouse}.

\subsubsection*{On-board control and state estimation}

All low-level control runs on the drone's microcontroller: the cascade-PID firmware
of Sections~\ref{sec:cascade_pid} and~\ref{sec:firmware_integration}, with its
position and velocity loops at $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$, its attitude and attitude-rate
loops at $500\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and a $1\,\mathrm{kHz}$ main loop. A MEMS inertial
measurement unit supplies three-axis gyroscope and accelerometer readings. The
gyroscope measures the body rates $\omega_B = (p,q,r)^\top$ of
Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} directly, so they feed the attitude-rate loop without any
further estimation; the accelerometer, together with the external positioning
measurements (Section~\ref{sec:flowdeck_lighthouse}), is fused by an on-board extended
Kalman filter into the remaining states (position, velocity and attitude). The measured
body rates and this filtered estimate together form the full state used by the controller
and recorded for logging. The drone accepts setpoints at any level of the cascade through
its radio protocol.

\subsubsection*{Host link and logging}

Each experiment is commanded from a host PC over a $2.4\,\mathrm{GHz}$
Crazyradio~2.0 USB link using the Bitcraze Python library: the PC streams setpoints
to the drone, and the drone streams its on-board state estimate (together with the
raw gyroscope) back, which is recorded for the offline comparison with simulation.
In this chapter no part of the controller runs on the PC --- the drone flies
entirely under its own firmware --- so the host acts purely as a command-and-
logging interface.

\subsection{Flowdeck vs Lighthouse}\label{sec:flowdeck_lighthouse}

On their own, the accelerometer and gyroscope cannot keep the position and velocity
estimates from drifting, as integrating inertial measurements accumulates error
unbounded over time. The on-board Kalman filter therefore relies on an external
positioning deck to remain accurate. Two were evaluated, each feeding the same filter:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \emph{Flowdeck}, a downward-facing deck that combines an optical-flow
    camera --- which senses horizontal velocity from the apparent motion of the
    ground --- with a time-of-flight laser rangefinder for height. It provides only a
    \emph{relative} reference: it constrains velocity and height but lets the absolute
    horizontal position drift over time;
    \item the \emph{Lighthouse}, which recovers an \emph{absolute} six-degree-of-freedom
    pose from the infrared sweeps of three Lighthouse~V2 base stations, picked up by
    an on-board receiver deck, at sub-millimetre and sub-degree accuracy.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Circle Trajectory: Simulation vs Reality}\label{sec:circle_simreal}

Having validated the \emph{dynamics} in open loop (Section~\ref{sec:dyn_comparison})
and ported the on-board controller to Python
(Section~\ref{sec:firmware_integration}), we now close the loop and ask the
decisive question for the rest of this work: does the simulator reproduce a real
flight faithfully enough to be trusted as a training ground? To answer it we fly
the same non-trivial manoeuvre --- a vertical takeoff, one full circle, and a
landing --- on the real Crazyflie and on its simulated twin, and compare the two
states. In both cases the drone is flown entirely by its own cascade-PID firmware
(the Python port for the simulation, the native firmware for the real platform);
no learned policy is involved yet, so any disagreement is attributable to the
fidelity of the simulator, not to a controller.

\subsubsection*{Protocol and two error measures}

The reference trajectory is generated identically on both sides: a
$2\,\mathrm{s}$ climb to a hover height of $1\,\mathrm{m}$, a
circle of radius $0.5\,\mathrm{m}$ traced over $6\,\mathrm{s}$, and a
$2\,\mathrm{s}$ descent, the whole path being rigidly recentred on the drone's
measured start position. Position setpoints are streamed at $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$
and the on-board state estimate is logged at $20\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The simulation
runs the same firmware cascade on the \texttt{PYB\_GND\_DRAG\_DW} physics
back-end (Section~\ref{sec:gym_pybullet}), with a first-order motor-lag filter
($\tau \approx 20\,\mathrm{ms}$) restoring the finite bandwidth of the real
brushed motors. All metrics are computed over the circle phase only
($2$--$8\,\mathrm{s}$), excluding the takeoff and landing transients.

It is essential to separate two quantities that are easily conflated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \emph{tracking error}, between the drone and its commanded
    setpoint. This measures how well the cascade PID follows the moving target;
    \item the \emph{sim-to-real discrepancy}, between the real state and the
    simulated state. This measures how faithfully the simulator reproduces the
    real platform, and is the actual object of this section.
\end{itemize}
The two are reported in Tables~\ref{tab:circle_tracking}
and~\ref{tab:circle_discrepancy} respectively. As will become clear, the
tracking error is dominated by an effect common to both simulation and reality,
whereas the discrepancy is small --- so a large tracking error and a faithful
simulator are perfectly compatible.

\subsubsection*{Flow deck (the originally available system)}

The laboratory was first equipped only with the optical-flow \emph{Flow deck},
so the experiment was initially run with it. The top view of the resulting flight
(Figure~\ref{fig:topview_flow}) already reveals its limitations: the logged
trajectory is a visibly distorted, jittery circle rather than the clean ring of
the setpoint. The time series (Figure~\ref{fig:ts_flowdeck}) confirms that, while
the position is broadly tracked, the estimated velocity and attitude are
markedly noisy. The effect is most violent during the takeoff and landing phases
(outside the green markers): there the estimate diverges into large spurious
spikes in horizontal velocity and yaw, because optical flow is ill-conditioned
close to the ground --- the apparent ground motion scales with altitude, and the
time-of-flight rangefinder operates near its lower limit. This near-ground
failure is precisely why, in the learning experiments (Section~\ref{sec:nn_results}),
takeoff and landing are always delegated to the cascade PID and the learned
manoeuvre is confined to a safe altitude.

The sim-to-real discrepancy for the Flow deck flight is correspondingly large:
$7.2\,\mathrm{cm}$ in position and $13.3\,\mathrm{cm/s}$ in velocity over the
circle (Table~\ref{tab:circle_discrepancy}), the velocity figure being roughly
twice the Lighthouse value. One caveat must be stated plainly: with the Flow
deck the logged ``real'' state \emph{is} the optical-flow estimate itself, for
which no independent ground truth is available. Part of the $7.2\,\mathrm{cm}$
therefore reflects the Flow deck mis-estimating its own position, not the drone
genuinely departing from the simulated path. Either way, the conclusion is the
same: the Flow deck is too unreliable to serve as a reference, which motivated
the acquisition of the Lighthouse system. The detailed reliability comparison is
deferred to Section~\ref{sec:flowdeck_lighthouse}.

\subsubsection*{Lighthouse (the system adopted for validation)}

With the Lighthouse system the picture changes completely. Its sub-millimetre,
sub-degree pose can be treated as ground truth, so the comparison is meaningful.
The top view (Figure~\ref{fig:topview_lh}) shows the real circle and its
simulated twin almost superimposed on the commanded ring, and the time series
(Figure~\ref{fig:ts_lighthouse}) agree closely across position, velocity and
attitude. Quantitatively, the sim-to-real discrepancy over the circle is only
$4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ in position --- about $8\,\%$ of the circle radius --- with a
$5.3\,\mathrm{cm}$ maximum, $6.3\,\mathrm{cm/s}$ in velocity and below
$0.75^\circ$ in roll and pitch (Table~\ref{tab:circle_discrepancy}). The
simulator is, to this accuracy, a faithful twin of the real platform.

\subsubsection*{Reading the numbers, and what they imply for training}

The tracking error deserves a word, because at $24$--$29\,\mathrm{cm}$ it looks
alarmingly large next to a $0.5\,\mathrm{m}$ radius. It is, however, almost
entirely an \emph{along-track} (phase) lag rather than a radial deviation: the
drone follows essentially the right circle but trails the moving target by some
$30^\circ$ of arc (about half a second at the circle's angular rate), which is
why the top views still show well-formed rings. Crucially this lag is a property
of the soft default position gains, not of the sensor, so it appears almost
identically in simulation and in reality --- and is therefore subtracted out in
the sim-to-real discrepancy. This is exactly the regime one hopes for: a
controller that lags its setpoint, but a simulator that reproduces that lag, so
that the discrepancy ($4\,\mathrm{cm}$) is an order of magnitude below the
tracking error ($29\,\mathrm{cm}$).

The residual sim-to-real discrepancy on the Lighthouse flight is small and
structured. The dominant contributions are a slightly noisier real attitude ---
unmodelled IMU and vibration noise, since the simulator carries only the
deterministic motor-lag filter --- and a slow, uncommanded drift of the yaw by a
few degrees, attributable to estimator drift and small airframe asymmetries.

Two consequences follow for the learning experiments. First, a sim-to-real
fidelity of $4\,\mathrm{cm}$ on a dynamic manoeuvre justifies training the neural
controller in simulation and expecting it to transfer, since the dominant flight
behaviour --- including the controller lag --- is reproduced. Second, the part of
the gap that the simulator does \emph{not} capture, namely the stochastic
sensor and attitude noise, is precisely the kind of perturbation against which a
policy must be made robust; this is the rationale for injecting noise during
training, which markedly improved convergence and transfer
(Section~\ref{sec:nn_training}).
