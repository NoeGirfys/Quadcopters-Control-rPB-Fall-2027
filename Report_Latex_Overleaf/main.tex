\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}

Autonomous quadcopters are increasingly deployed in applications that require reliable operation
under varying and uncertain conditions, such as inspection, delivery, or search and rescue.
A fundamental challenge in this context is that real-world flight is subject to disturbances
and parametric variations that are difficult to model precisely: wind gusts, uneven payloads,
sensor noise, or mechanical wear can significantly degrade the performance of a controller
designed under nominal assumptions.

Classical control approaches, such as proportional-integral-derivative (PID) cascades, are
widely used in practice due to their simplicity and robustness. However, their performance
degrades when the system deviates significantly from the conditions under which they were
tuned, and re-tuning for each new scenario is time-consuming and requires expert knowledge.
Learning-based approaches offer a more flexible alternative, as they can in principle adapt
to complex and unknown dynamics from data. Yet standard training procedures produce a fixed
controller that is no more adaptable than its classical counterpart once deployed: it cannot
efficiently incorporate the few observations available at runtime to adjust its behavior.

Meta-learning addresses this limitation by shifting the training objective from learning a
single high-performing policy to learning a policy initialization that can be rapidly
fine-tuned to a new task from a small number of gradient steps~\cite{finn2017maml}.
Applied to quadcopter control, this paradigm promises controllers that maintain good
performance not only in nominal conditions but also after fast in-flight adaptation to
previously unseen disturbances.

A second fundamental challenge, largely orthogonal to the controller design itself, is the
\emph{sim-to-real gap}: controllers trained in simulation rarely transfer directly to hardware
because the simulated dynamics do not exactly match those of the physical system.
Closing this gap requires a simulation environment that is faithful to the real platform,
which in turn requires careful modeling of the onboard control architecture.

\subsection{Prior Work}

This project builds directly on the work of C. Goffin~\cite{goffin2026}, who investigated the
application of Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) to neural network quadcopter controllers
in the preceding semester. Goffin formulated the control problem as the regulation of a
quadcopter to the origin in the presence of wind disturbances modeled as additive Gaussian
noise on the translational dynamics. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) policy was trained by differentiating a
cumulative quadratic cost through an unrolled simulation horizon, with the full control input
$(F, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z)$ as the network output. Both a linearized and a nonlinear
dynamics model were considered, and MAML was shown to outperform joint training when the
test disturbance distribution differs from the training one. All experiments were conducted
in simulation using a custom Python dynamics model; no real hardware was involved.

\subsection{Contributions}

The present work extends Goffin's framework in two complementary directions: making the
simulation faithful to a specific real platform, and deploying the resulting controllers
on actual hardware. The contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Simulation environment selection and validation.}
    The \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} library~\cite{panerati2021} is adopted as the
    simulation backend and its physics modes are compared against Goffin's dynamics model
    in open loop to verify consistency.

    \item \textbf{Crazyflie firmware integration.}
    The default PID controller provided by \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} does not match
    the cascade PID architecture embedded in the Crazyflie firmware. The official
    Crazyflie C firmware is ported to Python and integrated into the simulator, enabling
    closed-loop simulations that closely replicate the real drone's control pipeline.

    \item \textbf{Sim-to-real validation.}
    The fidelity of the simulation is evaluated experimentally by commanding the real
    Crazyflie to follow a circular trajectory and comparing the resulting flight with the
    simulated equivalent. Two positioning systems are compared: the optical flow Flowdeck
    and the Lighthouse system, with the latter retained for its superior accuracy.

    \item \textbf{Hybrid NN-PID architecture and training.}
    Because the Crazyflie firmware does not expose direct motor RPM commands at the
    required bandwidth, the neural network is designed to replace only the outer two levels
    of the cascade PID (position and velocity loops), with its outputs fed into the inner
    attitude and rate loops. This hybrid architecture is trained in simulation and
    evaluated both in simulation and on the real drone.

    \item \textbf{MAML for offset mass compensation.}
    MAML is applied to a disturbance type not considered by Goffin: an offset point mass
    attached to the drone body, which shifts the center of mass and modifies the effective
    inertia tensor. Indeed, the wind disturbance is very difficult to produce and control in
    laboratory, while we can easily stick a mass to the drone. This disturbance is persistent
    and asymmetric, making fast adaptation particularly valuable. The meta-trained controller
    is evaluated in simulation and on the real platform.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\subsection{Quadcopter Dynamics}

A mathematical model of the quadcopter is required to design the control policy and simulate
its behavior. The model used here follows the standard rigid-body mechanics framework
employed in~\cite{goffin2026}, but is adapted to the \emph{X~configuration} of the
Crazyflie platform, which differs from the \emph{$+$~configuration} used by Goffin.
As detailed below, this distinction changes the expressions for the body torques, since in
the X~configuration all four rotors contribute to both roll and pitch, whereas in the
$+$~configuration only two rotors govern each axis.

\subsubsection*{Reference frames}

Two reference frames are introduced (see \cref{fig:quadcopter}): the inertial frame
$(x_I, y_I, z_I)$ fixed to the ground, and the body frame $(x_B, y_B, z_B)$ rigidly
attached to the drone's center of mass, with $x_B$ pointing forward, $y_B$ to the left,
and $z_B$ upward. Roll, pitch, and yaw angles about $x_B$, $y_B$, $z_B$ are denoted
$\phi$, $\theta$, and $\psi$, respectively.
The rotation matrix from the body frame to the inertial frame follows the ZYX Euler
convention:
\begin{equation}
    R =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathrm{c}\theta\mathrm{c}\psi &
        \mathrm{s}\phi\mathrm{s}\theta\mathrm{c}\psi - \mathrm{c}\phi\mathrm{s}\psi &
        \mathrm{c}\phi\mathrm{s}\theta\mathrm{c}\psi + \mathrm{s}\phi\mathrm{s}\psi \\
        \mathrm{c}\theta\mathrm{s}\psi &
        \mathrm{s}\phi\mathrm{s}\theta\mathrm{s}\psi + \mathrm{c}\phi\mathrm{c}\psi &
        \mathrm{c}\phi\mathrm{s}\theta\mathrm{s}\psi - \mathrm{s}\phi\mathrm{c}\psi \\
        -\mathrm{s}\theta & \mathrm{s}\phi\mathrm{c}\theta & \mathrm{c}\phi\mathrm{c}\theta
    \end{bmatrix},
    \label{eq:rotation_matrix}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{c}(\cdot)$ and $\mathrm{s}(\cdot)$ denote cosine and sine, respectively.

\subsubsection*{Motor forces, X~configuration, and body torques}

Each rotor $i\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ spinning at angular speed $\omega_i$ produces an upward
thrust $F_i = k_t\,\omega_i^2$ and a reactive drag torque $k_d\,\omega_i^2$, where $k_t$
and $k_d$ are the thrust and drag coefficients.

In the X~configuration, no rotor is aligned with a body axis; instead, each rotor is
offset by $45°$ from $x_B$ and $y_B$.
Denoting $a = L/\sqrt{2}$ the projected arm length, the body-frame positions of the four
rotors are:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{r}_1 = \begin{pmatrix}+a\\-a\end{pmatrix},\quad
    \mathbf{r}_2 = \begin{pmatrix}-a\\-a\end{pmatrix},\quad
    \mathbf{r}_3 = \begin{pmatrix}-a\\+a\end{pmatrix},\quad
    \mathbf{r}_4 = \begin{pmatrix}+a\\+a\end{pmatrix},
    \label{eq:motor_positions}
\end{equation}
where the components are $(x_B, y_B)$. The roll and pitch torques are obtained by
computing the moment of each thrust force about the center of mass:
\begin{align}
    \tau_x &= \sum_{i=1}^{4} r_{y,i}\,F_i
             = \frac{k_t L}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(-\omega_1^2 - \omega_2^2 + \omega_3^2 + \omega_4^2\bigr),
             \label{eq:tau_x}\\
    \tau_y &= -\sum_{i=1}^{4} r_{x,i}\,F_i
             = \frac{k_t L}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(-\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2 + \omega_3^2 - \omega_4^2\bigr).
             \label{eq:tau_y}
\end{align}
Motors M1 and M3 spin counter-clockwise, while M2 and M4 spin clockwise.
The resulting yaw torque is:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_z = k_d\bigl(-\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2 - \omega_3^2 + \omega_4^2\bigr).
    \label{eq:tau_z}
\end{equation}

Note that in the $+$~configuration used by Goffin~\cite{goffin2026}, each axis is
controlled by only two motors (e.g.\ $\tau_x = k_t l (\omega_1^2 - \omega_3^2)$),
whereas in the X~configuration, \emph{all four motors} contribute to both $\tau_x$
and $\tau_y$.

\subsubsection*{Nonlinear dynamics}

Applying Newton's second law in the inertial frame and Euler's rigid-body theorem in the
body frame yields the following nonlinear equations of motion:
\begin{align}
    m\ddot{x} &= F\bigl(\cos\phi\sin\theta\cos\psi + \sin\phi\sin\psi\bigr), \label{eq:nl_x}\\
    m\ddot{y} &= F\bigl(\cos\phi\sin\theta\sin\psi - \sin\phi\cos\psi\bigr), \label{eq:nl_y}\\
    m\ddot{z} &= F\cos\phi\cos\theta - mg, \label{eq:nl_z}\\
    I_x\ddot{\phi}   &= \tau_x + (I_y - I_z)\dot{\theta}\dot{\psi}, \label{eq:nl_phi}\\
    I_y\ddot{\theta} &= \tau_y + (I_z - I_x)\dot{\phi}\dot{\psi}, \label{eq:nl_theta}\\
    I_z\ddot{\psi}   &= \tau_z + (I_x - I_y)\dot{\phi}\dot{\theta}, \label{eq:nl_psi}
\end{align}
where $F = \sum_{i=1}^{4} F_i$ is the total thrust, $m$ the drone mass, $g$ the
gravitational acceleration, and $I_x$, $I_y$, $I_z$ the principal moments of inertia.
These translational and rotational equations are independent of the motor configuration;
only the expressions for $\tau_x$, $\tau_y$, $\tau_z$ differ between the X and $+$ layouts.

\begin{rmk}
Equations~\eqref{eq:nl_phi}--\eqref{eq:nl_psi} are written in terms of Euler angle
derivatives $\dot{\phi}$, $\dot{\theta}$, $\dot{\psi}$.
Euler's rigid-body theorem strictly involves the body-frame angular rates $(p,q,r)$,
which are related to Euler angle rates through a kinematic transformation matrix.
Near hover ($\phi \approx 0$, $\theta \approx 0$), this matrix reduces to the identity,
so $p \approx \dot{\phi}$, $q \approx \dot{\theta}$, $r \approx \dot{\psi}$, and
the above form is valid.
The \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} simulator avoids this approximation entirely by
integrating orientation via unit quaternions, which remains exact at all attitudes.
\end{rmk}

\subsubsection*{Linearization around hover}

Near the hovering equilibrium ($\phi \approx \theta \approx 0$,
$\psi = \mathrm{const}$, zero velocities), applying the small-angle approximation
($\sin\alpha \approx \alpha$, $\cos\alpha \approx 1$) and setting the hover thrust
$F_0 = mg$, the dynamics simplify to:
\begin{alignat}{2}
    \ddot{x} &= \frac{F_0}{m}\,\theta, &\qquad
    \ddot{\phi}   &= \frac{\tau_x}{I_x}, \\
    \ddot{y} &= -\frac{F_0}{m}\,\phi,  &\qquad
    \ddot{\theta} &= \frac{\tau_y}{I_y}, \\
    \ddot{z} &= \frac{\delta F}{m},    &\qquad
    \ddot{\psi}   &= \frac{\tau_z}{I_z},
\end{alignat}
where $\delta F = F - mg$ is the thrust deviation from hover. These equations are of the
same form as in~\cite{goffin2026}; the X~configuration enters only through the expressions
for $\tau_x$, $\tau_y$, $\tau_z$ given in \cref{eq:tau_x,eq:tau_y,eq:tau_z}.

\subsubsection*{State-space representation and discretization}

Defining the state vector
$\mathbf{x} = \bigl[x,\,\dot{x},\,y,\,\dot{y},\,z,\,\dot{z},\,
\phi,\,\dot{\phi},\,\theta,\,\dot{\theta},\,\psi,\,\dot{\psi}\bigr]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{12}$
and control input $\mathbf{u} = \bigl[F,\,\tau_x,\,\tau_y,\,\tau_z\bigr]^\top$,
the linearized continuous-time dynamics take the standard form
$\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} + B\mathbf{u}$.

For numerical simulation, the continuous model is discretized with sampling period $T_s$.
The linearized model uses a zero-order hold (ZOH) on the input:
$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A_d\,\mathbf{x}_k + B_d\,\mathbf{u}_k$.
The nonlinear model uses a forward Euler scheme:
$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + T_s\,f(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k)$,
where $f(\cdot)$ collects the right-hand sides of
\cref{eq:nl_x,eq:nl_y,eq:nl_z,eq:nl_phi,eq:nl_theta,eq:nl_psi}.

\subsection{Crazyflie Cascade PID Architecture}
\label{sec:cascade_pid}

The Crazyflie 2.1 onboard firmware controls the quadcopter through a \emph{cascade PID}
architecture, in which four nested feedback loops operate at different rates and pass
their outputs as setpoints to the loop below.
This layered design separates the slow position dynamics from the fast rotational dynamics,
enabling the use of different gains and update frequencies at each level.
A precise Python replica of this architecture was implemented in this work
(as the default \texttt{gym-pybullet-drones} controller does not match the real firmware)
and is used throughout the simulation and training pipeline.

\subsubsection*{Loop hierarchy and frequencies}

The four levels, from outermost to innermost, are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Position loop} (100\,Hz): converts a position setpoint
          $(x^*, y^*, z^*)$ into a velocity setpoint $(\dot{x}^*, \dot{y}^*, \dot{z}^*)$
          using three independent PID controllers, one per axis.

    \item \textbf{Velocity loop} (100\,Hz): converts the velocity setpoint into
          a scalar thrust command $T \in [0, 65535]$ (16-bit PWM units) and desired
          roll and pitch angles $(\phi^*, \theta^*)$ in degrees. Yaw is handled
          separately as a desired yaw rate $\dot{\psi}^*$.

    \item \textbf{Attitude loop} (500\,Hz): compares the desired angles
          $(\phi^*, \theta^*, \psi^*)$ to the measured attitude and outputs
          desired body rates $(p^*, q^*, r^*)$ in degrees per second.

    \item \textbf{Rate loop} (500\,Hz): compares the desired body rates to the
          measured gyroscope rates and outputs three torque commands
          $(\delta_{\mathrm{roll}}, \delta_{\mathrm{pitch}}, \delta_{\mathrm{yaw}})$,
          which are summed with the thrust command to produce individual motor PWM signals.
\end{enumerate}

The position and velocity loops share the 100\,Hz update rate (POSITION\_RATE); the
attitude and rate loops run at 500\,Hz (ATTITUDE\_RATE). For every one step of the outer
loops, five steps of the inner loops are executed.

\subsubsection*{Motor mixing}

The four per-motor PWM values are assembled from the torque commands by the motor mixing
table, which encodes the X~configuration geometry derived in \cref{eq:tau_x,eq:tau_y,eq:tau_z}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}\text{PWM}_1\\\text{PWM}_2\\\text{PWM}_3\\\text{PWM}_4\end{pmatrix}
    = T\,\mathbf{1}
    + \frac{\delta_{\mathrm{roll}}}{2}
      \begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\+1\\+1\end{pmatrix}
    + \frac{\delta_{\mathrm{pitch}}}{2}
      \begin{pmatrix}+1\\-1\\-1\\+1\end{pmatrix}
    + \delta_{\mathrm{yaw}}
      \begin{pmatrix}+1\\-1\\+1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:motor_mixing}
\end{equation}
If the highest PWM value exceeds the 16-bit ceiling (65535), all four channels are
reduced by the same amount so the highest channel saturates rather than overflows.
The resulting PWM signals are converted to rotor RPMs through the thrust–RPM curve
of the CF2.1+ brushless motors; a first-order lag with time constant $\tau_m = 20\,\mathrm{ms}$
models the motor spin-up dynamics.

\subsubsection*{Role in the hybrid architecture}

The neural network controller introduced in this work \emph{replaces the two outer loops}
(position and velocity) while keeping the two inner loops (attitude and rate) exactly as in
the real firmware. At each 100\,Hz step the network receives the full 12-dimensional state
and outputs the tuple $(T, \phi^*, \theta^*, \dot{\psi}^*)$, which is then handed
to the 500\,Hz PID chain for the remaining five rate-loop substeps.
This design is motivated by the limited bandwidth of the communication interface with the
real Crazyflie: direct motor RPM commands cannot be sent at the required rate, but desired
attitude setpoints can. The inner firmware loops thus act as a hardware-level actuator
abstraction, and the learning problem is confined to the outer dynamics.

\subsection{Neural Network Control Policy}
\label{sec:policy}

The neural network controller is a multilayer perceptron (MLP) that replaces the
position and velocity PID loops described in \cref{sec:cascade_pid}.
At each 100\,Hz step it receives the current state and a desired position and outputs
the setpoint tuple $(T, \phi^*, \theta^*, \dot{\psi}^*)$ consumed by the inner firmware PID chain.

\subsubsection*{Inputs and outputs}

The network input is the concatenation of two parts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the 12-dimensional state
          $\mathbf{x} = [x,\dot{x},y,\dot{y},z,\dot{z},\phi,\dot{\phi},\theta,\dot{\theta},\psi,\dot{\psi}]^\top$,
          normalized element-wise by a fixed scale vector $\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{12}$
          chosen to bring typical ranges to the order of unity;
    \item the 3-dimensional relative target
          $\Delta\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{p}^* - [x,y,z]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^3$,
          where $\mathbf{p}^*$ is the desired position.
\end{itemize}

The four outputs are bounded through saturating non-linearities to enforce physical validity:
\begin{align}
    T       &= \sigma(\hat{T}) \cdot T_{\max},     \label{eq:out_thrust}\\
    \phi^*  &= \tanh(\hat{\phi})\cdot\phi_{\max},  \label{eq:out_roll}\\
    \theta^*&= \tanh(\hat{\theta})\cdot\theta_{\max}, \label{eq:out_pitch}\\
    \dot{\psi}^* &= \tanh(\hat{\psi})\cdot\dot{\psi}_{\max}, \label{eq:out_yaw}
\end{align}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic sigmoid, and $(\hat{T}, \hat{\phi}, \hat{\theta}, \hat{\psi})$
are the raw network outputs.
The sigmoid ensures the thrust command $T\in[0, T_{\max}]$ is non-negative,
while $\tanh$ bounds the attitude setpoints symmetrically.
Limits are set to $T_{\max} = 65535$ (full 16-bit PWM range),
$\phi_{\max} = \theta_{\max} = 20°$, and $\dot{\psi}_{\max} = 200°/\mathrm{s}$,
matching the velocity-loop output limits of the real firmware.

\subsubsection*{Architecture}

Both the classic-regulation and the MAML controllers share the same backbone:
a three-layer fully connected network with $\tanh$ activations in the hidden layers
and a linear output layer:
\begin{equation}
    f_\theta(\mathbf{z}) =
    W_3\,\tanh\bigl(W_2\,\tanh(W_1 \mathbf{z} + b_1) + b_2\bigr) + b_3,
    \quad \mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{15},
    \label{eq:mlp}
\end{equation}
with 64 hidden units per layer.

The last-layer bias is initialized so that the network outputs the hover thrust at startup
regardless of the input state: since $\sigma(b_3^{(0)}) = T_{\mathrm{hover}}/T_{\max}$,
a logit of the hover ratio is used, and all other output biases are set to zero.
This warm-start prevents the drone from crashing in the first training rollouts.

\subsubsection*{Concurrent variant for classic regulation}

To reduce the number of network evaluations per training trajectory, the
classic-regulation policy uses a \emph{concurrent} formulation following
Wiedemann et al.~(ICRA 2023): the network is queried \emph{once per chunk} of
$T$ time steps and outputs all $T$ future setpoints simultaneously.
The input is augmented to include the $T$ relative targets for each upcoming step
(all identical for the regulation-to-origin task), yielding an input dimension of
$12 + 3T$ and an output dimension of $4T$.
For the MAML policy the chunk size is fixed to $T = 1$, reverting to the
standard single-step formulation.

\subsection{Training by Differentiation Through Simulation}
\label{sec:bptt}

\subsubsection*{Analytic policy gradient}

Training the network requires computing gradients of a scalar cost with respect to the
policy parameters $\theta$. In standard reinforcement learning this gradient is estimated
by sampling; here it is computed \emph{analytically} by differentiating the cost through
the entire simulated trajectory, a technique known as \emph{back-propagation through time}
(BPTT)~\cite{goffin2026}.

Concretely, starting from a batch of $B$ initial states $\{\mathbf{x}_0^{(b)}\}$,
a trajectory of $N_{\mathrm{step}}$ states is collected by unrolling the closed-loop system:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{(b)} = f_{\mathrm{sim}}\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}_k^{(b)},\,
    \pi_\theta(\mathbf{x}_k^{(b)}, \mathbf{p}^*)\bigr),
    \label{eq:unroll}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\mathrm{sim}}$ comprises the attitude/rate PID chain followed by one
dynamics substep, and $\pi_\theta$ is the policy network. The training loss is a
cumulative quadratic cost:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
    \left[
        \sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{x}_k^{(b)\top} Q\, \mathbf{x}_k^{(b)}
        + w_T \|\mathbf{p}_{N}^{(b)}\|^2
    \right],
    \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is a positive-definite weighting matrix (with heavier penalties on positions
and angles), and $w_T$ is a terminal position weight. Because all operations in
\cref{eq:unroll} are differentiable PyTorch operations, \texttt{loss.backward()}
propagates gradients of $\mathcal{L}$ through the entire unrolled computation graph
back to $\theta$, and parameters are updated with Adam.

Compared to model-free approaches, this analytic gradient is low-variance and
sample-efficient, but requires a differentiable simulator. Note that the gradient
must traverse the PID chain: the attitude and rate loops are therefore implemented
as differentiable PyTorch functions with no \texttt{detach} calls.

\subsubsection*{Curriculum reset}

A practical obstacle in training long trajectories is that the drone may diverge
early (large initial error drives it far from the origin), producing vanishing or
exploding gradients from distant time steps. A \emph{curriculum reset} strategy
is introduced to address this: at each training step, any drone in the batch whose
3D position error exceeds a threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{div}}$ is reset to the origin
and its PID integrators are re-initialized.

The threshold grows linearly over the $E$ training epochs:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{div}}(e) = \tau_{\mathrm{start}}
    + \frac{e}{E - 1}\,(\tau_{\mathrm{end}} - \tau_{\mathrm{start}}),
    \quad e = 0,\ldots,E-1.
    \label{eq:curriculum}
\end{equation}
Early in training ($\tau_{\mathrm{div}}$ small) the drone is reset frequently,
keeping trajectories short and gradients well-conditioned. As training progresses,
$\tau_{\mathrm{div}}$ increases, allowing the drone to wander further before a
reset, until by the final epoch it can sustain the full nominal trajectory without
intervention.

\subsection{Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML)}
\label{sec:maml}

\subsubsection*{Problem setting}

Meta-learning addresses the problem of learning to learn: instead of training a single
fixed policy for one task, we train an initialization $\theta$ that can be rapidly
adapted to any task from a small amount of data~\cite{finn2017maml}.
Formally, a \emph{task} $\mathcal{T}_i$ is a distribution over initial states and
a dynamics parameterization (e.g., a different attached mass); the meta-learner is
given access to a distribution $p(\mathcal{T})$ over tasks at train time and must
produce a policy that, after a \emph{few gradient steps} on a new task drawn from
$p(\mathcal{T})$, achieves low cost on that task.

\subsubsection*{Algorithm}

Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML, Finn et al. 2017) solves this by optimizing
the parameters $\theta$ of the shared policy network so that the \emph{adapted}
parameters $\theta'$, obtained by a small number of gradient descent steps on each
task's support data, perform well on that task's query data.
At each meta-training epoch, given $M$ tasks $\{\mathcal{T}_i\}_{i=1}^M$:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Inner loop} (task adaptation):
    for each task $i$, compute adapted parameters $\theta_i'$ by taking
    $N_{\mathrm{inner}}$ gradient steps with step size $\alpha$ on the
    support loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}^{\mathrm{sup}}$:
    \begin{equation}
        \theta_i' = \theta - \alpha\,\nabla_\theta\,
        \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}^{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta).
        \label{eq:inner}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Outer loop} (meta-update):
    update the shared initialization $\theta$ to minimize the sum of query
    losses evaluated at the adapted parameters $\theta_i'$:
    \begin{equation}
        \theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta - \beta\,\nabla_\theta
        \sum_{i=1}^{M} \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}^{\mathrm{qry}}(\theta_i').
        \label{eq:outer}
    \end{equation}

\end{enumerate}

The support and query sets are disjoint batches of initial states.
In the outer update the gradient $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_i')$ passes
\emph{through} the inner update of \cref{eq:inner}, which requires computing
second-order derivatives; this is the full (2nd-order) MAML implementation
used here. A first-order approximation (FOMAML) is also supported by detaching
the inner-loop gradient from the meta-computation graph, at the cost of a biased
but cheaper meta-gradient.

\subsubsection*{Adaptation at test time}

At deployment, the network is adapted to a new (unseen) task by simply
repeating \cref{eq:inner} for $N_{\mathrm{adapt}}$ steps on a small support
batch of trajectories collected on that task, without any further meta-update.
The adapted parameters $\theta'$ are then used for inference.
Because the network is small (64 hidden units) and only a few gradient steps are
needed, adaptation is fast enough to run before each real flight.
